\chapter{Pre-Training, Scaling Laws, and the Evolution of Large Language Models}
\label{ch:scaling_laws_pretraining}

Chapter~\ref{ch:transformer_variants} analyzed the structural taxonomy of Transformer variants, detailing how encoder-only, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only topologies specialize for distinct linguistic representations and sequence generation tasks. While architectural design dictates how tokens interact within the self-attention mechanism, the emergent cognitive and linguistic capabilities of modern foundation models are governed by the volume of self-supervised pre-training, the scale of parameters, and the total compute allocated during optimization.

The evolution of modern large language models began with the recognition that unsupervised autoregressive pre-training over diverse text corpora provides a universal initialization that transfers seamlessly to downstream tasks. Subsequent scaling experiments revealed that language model performance adheres to quantitative empirical power laws, triggering an industry-wide race toward massive parameter regimes. However, subsequent empirical evaluations demonstrated that simply increasing parameter counts without proportionally scaling training tokens produces severely undertrained models, establishing the compute-optimal scaling frontier and laying the foundation for the modern open-weight ecosystem.

This chapter provides an exhaustive theoretical and empirical analysis of the evolution of large language models, their pre-training methodologies, and the scaling laws governing their performance. We begin in Section~\ref{sec:gpt1_architecture} by formalizing the foundation of the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT-1), dissecting its two-stage pre-training and supervised fine-tuning framework with delimiter tokens. Section~\ref{sec:gpt2_scaling_laws} examines GPT-2, its zero-shot multi-task paradigm, WebText corpus curation, and the breakthrough empirical scaling laws of Kaplan et al. Section~\ref{sec:gpt3_in_context_learning} deconstructs GPT-3, contrasting supervised fine-tuning with in-context learning across zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot regimes. Section~\ref{sec:chinchilla_scaling} analyzes the race to massive scale, the undertraining diagnosis, and the three empirical approaches of the DeepMind Chinchilla framework that formalized compute-optimal pre-training. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:open_models_paradigm} examines the paradigm shift toward token saturation and the rise of modern open foundation models.


\section{GPT-1: Generative Pre-Training and Supervised Adaptation}
\label{sec:gpt1_architecture}

Prior to 2018, natural language processing was characterized by fragmented, task-specific architectures. Researchers predominantly trained recurrent neural networks (LSTMs, GRUs) or convolutional networks on supervised datasets from scratch, relying on static word embeddings such as Word2Vec or GloVe for initialization. While semi-supervised approaches such as ELMo contextualized word representations, the underlying task-specific model architectures still required bespoke engineering for each target task.

Radford et al.~\cite{radford2018} resolved this architectural fragmentation by introducing the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT-1). GPT-1 demonstrated that a single, universal task-agnostic Transformer architecture could be pre-trained using self-supervised causal language modeling on unannotated text, followed by minimal supervised fine-tuning on labeled downstream benchmarks.


\subsection{Architectural topology and experimental setup}
\label{subsec:gpt1_setup}

The core architecture of GPT-1 adapts the canonical Transformer decoder proposed by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017}, excising the encoder-decoder cross-attention sub-layers because no external source sequence is encoded. The model consists of a 12-layer decoder-only stack with the following dimensional specifications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Layer and Head Configuration}: $L = 12$ Transformer decoder blocks, each containing $H = 12$ self-attention heads with per-head projection dimension $d_{\text{head}} = d_{\text{model}} / H = 64$.
    \item \textbf{Hidden Dimensionality}: Hidden representation dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 768$, with position-wise feed-forward intermediate dimensionality $d_{\text{ff}} = 3072$ (a $4\times$ expansion ratio).
    \item \textbf{Parameter Scale}: Total parameter count of $N \approx 117\text{M}$ parameters.
    \item \textbf{Tokenization and Embeddings}: Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) subword tokenization with a vocabulary of 40,000 merge operations. Rather than fixed sinusoidal encodings, GPT-1 employs learned absolute positional embeddings up to a maximum context window of $L = 512$ tokens.
\end{itemize}

For its pre-training corpus, GPT-1 utilized the BooksCorpus dataset. Comprising approximately 7,000 unique unpublished books (amounting to $\approx 5\text{ GB}$ of text) across diverse literary genres (adventure, science fiction, romance), BooksCorpus provided continuous spans of long-form, coherent prose. This allowed the self-attention mechanism to learn long-range semantic and syntactic dependencies across contiguous discourse, rather than sentence-level fragments.


\subsection{Stage 1: Unsupervised autoregressive pre-training}
\label{subsec:gpt1_pretraining}

In the first stage of the GPT-1 framework, the network is trained on an unannotated corpus of tokens $\mathcal{U} = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N)$ via standard autoregressive language modeling. Given a conditioning context window of size $k$, the network maximizes the causal log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_1(\mathcal{U}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log P(u_i \mid u_{i-k}, \dots, u_{i-1}; \boldsymbol{\Theta}),
\label{eq:gpt1_unsupervised_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ denotes the complete parameter tensor of the Transformer decoder.

Figure~\ref{fig:gpt1_pretraining} illustrates the forward computation. Formally, given an input token sequence $(u_{i-k}, \dots, u_{i-1})$, the zeroth representation layer $\mathbf{h}_0$ is constructed by summing token embedding matrix $\mathbf{W}_e \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d_{\text{model}}}$ and positional embedding matrix $\mathbf{W}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_{\text{model}}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{W}_e + \mathbf{W}_p,
\label{eq:gpt1_input_representation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U} \in \{0, 1\}^{k \times |\mathcal{V}|}$ is the one-hot context matrix.

The representation is then propagated through $L$ identical Transformer decoder layers:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_l = \text{TransformerBlock}(\mathbf{h}_{l-1}), \quad \forall l \in \{1, \dots, L\}.
\label{eq:gpt1_layer_pass}
\end{equation}
The conditional probability distribution over vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ at step $i$ is obtained by projecting the final hidden state $\mathbf{h}_L$ through the transposed token embedding matrix $\mathbf{W}_e^\top$ (weight tying) or an explicit unembedding matrix $\mathbf{W}_U \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d_{\text{model}}}$:
\begin{equation}
P(u_i \mid u_{i-k}, \dots, u_{i-1}) = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{W}_U \mathbf{h}_{L, i-1} + \mathbf{b}_U).
\label{eq:gpt1_output_prob}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Stage 2: Supervised task adaptation and delimiter token linearization}
\label{subsec:gpt1_finetuning}

Once pre-training is complete, the parameters $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ provide a general-purpose semantic initialization. In the supervised fine-tuning stage, the model is adapted to a labeled dataset $\mathcal{C}$, where each instance consists of an input sequence of tokens $(x_1, \dots, x_m)$ and a ground-truth target label $y$.

The input sequence is passed through the pre-trained Transformer decoder to obtain the final activation vector of the terminal token, $\mathbf{h}_{L, m} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$. This state is fed into a lightweight linear classification layer parameterized by $\mathbf{W}_y \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_{\text{model}}}$:
\begin{equation}
P(y \mid x_1, \dots, x_m) = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{W}_y \mathbf{h}_{L, m} + \mathbf{b}_y).
\label{eq:gpt1_supervised_prob}
\end{equation}
The supervised objective minimizes the empirical cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_2(\mathcal{C}) = -\sum_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \in \mathcal{C}} \log P(y \mid x_1, \dots, x_m).
\label{eq:gpt1_supervised_loss}
\end{equation}

Critically, Radford et al. observed that incorporating an auxiliary language modeling objective during fine-tuning accelerates convergence, prevents catastrophic forgetting, and improves downstream generalization:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_3(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{L}_2(\mathcal{C}) + \lambda \mathcal{L}_1(\mathcal{C}),
\label{eq:gpt1_joint_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ balances supervised classification and self-supervised causal modeling (typically set to $\lambda = 0.5$).

A defining innovation of GPT-1 was the avoidance of custom architectural heads for structured inputs. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:gpt1_input_transformations}, complex multi-part inputs are linearized into continuous sequences using special delimiter tokens:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Text Classification}: The input sequence is bounded by start and extract tokens: $(\texttt{Start}, x_1, \dots, x_m, \texttt{Extract})$. The linear head reads the final contextual representation corresponding to \texttt{Extract}.
    \item \textbf{Natural Language Inference (NLI)}: Premise and hypothesis sequences are concatenated with a middle delimiter: $(\texttt{Start}, \text{Premise}, \texttt{Delim}, \text{Hypothesis}, \texttt{Extract})$.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Similarity}: Because similarity is symmetric, the pair is processed in both forward and reversed order: $(\texttt{Start}, \text{Text}_1, \texttt{Delim}, \text{Text}_2, \texttt{Extract})$ and $(\texttt{Start}, \text{Text}_2, \texttt{Delim}, \text{Text}_1, \texttt{Extract})$. The two resulting extract vectors $\mathbf{h}_{L, \text{ext}}^{(1)}$ and $\mathbf{h}_{L, \text{ext}}^{(2)}$ are summed element-wise before classification, ensuring permutation invariance.
    \item \textbf{Question Answering and Commonsense Reasoning}: For a context document $z$, question $q$, and set of candidate answers $\{a_k\}$, the model forms $K$ independent sequences: $(\texttt{Start}, z, \texttt{Delim}, q, \texttt{Delim}, a_k, \texttt{Extract})$. Each sequence produces an extract logit, and a softmax over all $K$ candidates yields the choice probability.
\end{enumerate}


\section{GPT-2 and Empirical Scaling Laws}
\label{sec:gpt2_scaling_laws}

Following the success of GPT-1, Radford et al.~\cite{radford2019} released GPT-2. The primary conceptual breakthrough of GPT-2 was the demonstration of \emph{unsupervised multi-task learning}: when trained on a sufficiently diverse and massive corpus, a language model develops zero-shot capabilities across distinct tasks without requiring supervised fine-tuning or parameter updates.


\subsection{Architectural stabilization and model scaling}
\label{subsec:gpt2_setup}

While preserving the core causal decoder topology of GPT-1, GPT-2 introduced critical structural modifications to stabilize optimization across deeper layer hierarchies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Layer Normalization (Pre-LN)}: In GPT-1, Layer Normalization was applied after the residual addition (\emph{Post-LN}): $\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \text{LN}(\mathbf{x}_l + \text{SubBlock}(\mathbf{x}_l))$. In GPT-2, normalization was shifted to the input pathway of each sub-block (\emph{Pre-LN}):
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \mathbf{x}_l + \text{SubBlock}(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}_l)).
    \label{eq:pre_layer_norm}
    \end{equation}
    An additional Layer Normalization layer was added following the final self-attention block prior to the unembedding projection. Pre-LN establishes an unhindered identity highway through the residual stream, preventing gradient vanishing or explosion in deep architectures.
    
    \item \textbf{Residual Projection Scaling}: To prevent variance accumulation across stacked residual connections at initialization, the weights of residual projection layers ($\mathbf{W}^O$ in self-attention and $\mathbf{W}_2$ in feed-forward networks) were scaled down by a depth-dependent factor:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{2N}}\right),
    \label{eq:residual_scaling}
    \end{equation}
    where $N$ is the number of Transformer layers.
    
    \item \textbf{Context and Vocabulary Expansion}: The context window was doubled from 512 to $L = 1024$ tokens. To handle arbitrary Unicode sequences without out-of-vocabulary (\texttt{[UNK]}) tokens, GPT-2 introduced Byte-Level Byte-Pair Encoding (BBPE) with a vocabulary of $|\mathcal{V}| = 50,257$.
\end{itemize}

GPT-2 was released as a family of four model sizes, scaling from 117M to 1.5B parameters:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GPT-2 Small}: $L = 12$ layers, $H = 12$ heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 768$ ($117\text{M}$ parameters, identical to GPT-1).
    \item \textbf{GPT-2 Medium}: $L = 24$ layers, $H = 16$ heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 1024$ ($345\text{M}$ parameters).
    \item \textbf{GPT-2 Large}: $L = 36$ layers, $H = 20$ heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 1280$ ($762\text{M}$ parameters).
    \item \textbf{GPT-2 Extra Large}: $L = 48$ layers, $H = 25$ heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 1600$ ($1.5\text{B}$ parameters).
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{WebText corpus curation and social upvote heuristic}
\label{subsec:gpt2_webtext}

A critical factor in GPT-2's emergent capabilities was the curation of the \emph{WebText} corpus. While common web-scraping repositories (such as Common Crawl) contained hundreds of terabytes of data, raw web scrapes were plagued by automated spam, duplicate content, and incoherent text.

To ensure high quality without manual labeling, Radford et al. designed a novel heuristic curation strategy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reddit Link Scraping}: Outbound links shared on the social news platform Reddit were extracted.
    \item \textbf{Human Karma Filtering}: Only links from Reddit posts receiving at least 3 karma (upvotes) were retained. This used human curation as an explicit heuristic filter: the Reddit community effectively pre-screened outbound web content for coherence, informative value, and linguistic quality.
    \item \textbf{Text Extraction and Deduplication}: From over 45 million links, Wikipedia articles were removed (to avoid contamination of evaluation benchmarks), HTML was stripped, and text was deduplicated.
\end{enumerate}
The resulting WebText dataset yielded approximately 40 GB of cleaned text—an $8\times$ expansion in text volume over BooksCorpus.


\subsection{Emergent zero-shot task capabilities and staged release}
\label{subsec:gpt2_zeroshot}

The central empirical result of GPT-2 was that language models, without any fine-tuning, can execute NLP tasks zero-shot by framing the objective as conditional natural language generation:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{Target} \mid \text{Context}, \text{Task Description}).
\label{eq:zero_shot_conditional}
\end{equation}
For example, appending the prompt delimiter \texttt{"TL;DR:"} to a document induced abstractive summarization; appending \texttt{"Q: ... A:"} prompted open-domain question answering; and prompting with bilingual phrase pairs prompted machine translation.

Radford et al. observed that downstream task performance improved in a log-linear relationship with parameter capacity: larger models consistently outperformed smaller models across reading comprehension, translation, and summarization without parameter updates.

Due to concerns regarding the potential misuse of 1.5B-scale language models for automated disinformation and synthetic content generation, OpenAI initially withheld the full model weights, opting for a phased, staged release of increasingly larger checkpoints over the course of 2019. This marked a historical shift from open-access scientific publishing toward risk-conscious governance and commercial closure in foundation model research.


\subsection{The breakthrough of empirical scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020)}
\label{subsec:kaplan_scaling_laws}

The continuous performance improvements observed when scaling from GPT-1 to GPT-2 prompted researchers at OpenAI to study whether language model loss followed predictable mathematical regularities. In January 2020, Kaplan et al.~\cite{kaplan2020} published the landmark paper \emph{"Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models"}.

Kaplan et al. systematically evaluated Transformer language models spanning more than four orders of magnitude in parameter count ($10^3$ to $10^9$ parameters) and compute budget ($10^{17}$ to $10^{23}$ floating-point operations, or FLOPs). Their investigations established that cross-entropy test loss $L$ scales as a power law with model parameter count $N$, dataset size $D$, and compute budget $C$:
\begin{align}
L(N) &\approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \quad \text{for } D \gg D_{\text{opt}}(N), \label{eq:kaplan_N} \\
L(D) &\approx \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}, \quad \text{for } N \gg N_{\text{opt}}(D), \label{eq:kaplan_D} \\
L(C) &\approx \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{\alpha_C}, \quad \text{for optimal allocation of } (N, D), \label{eq:kaplan_C}
\end{align}
where $N_c, D_c, C_c$ are constant scale factors, and the fitted power-law exponents were:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_N \approx 0.076, \quad \alpha_D \approx 0.057, \quad \alpha_C \approx 0.050.
\label{eq:kaplan_exponents}
\end{equation}

            \item \textbf{Parameter Scaling}: $N \propto C^{0.73}$.
            \item \textbf{Batch Scaling}: $B \propto C^{0.24}$.
            \item \textbf{Token / Step Scaling}: $S \propto C^{0.03}$ (tokens $D \propto C^{0.27}$).
            \item \textbf{Prescription}: As compute budget $C$ increases by $10\times$, scale model size $N$ by $5.4\times$, while increasing training tokens $D$ by only $1.8\times$.
        \end{itemize}
    };
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Key findings from the Kaplan et al. empirical scaling laws \cite{kaplan2020}. Cross-entropy loss scales smoothly as a power-law, showing strong insensitivity to architectural shape but prescribing an asymmetric allocation that prioritized parameter growth over dataset scaling.}
\label{fig:kaplan_takeaways}
\end{figure}

As summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:kaplan_takeaways}, the Kaplan scaling laws led to two major takeaways that shaped the field:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Insensitivity}: Test loss depends weakly on shape hyperparameters (such as network depth versus width, feed-forward dimension, or number of attention heads) for a constant total parameter count $N$.
    \item \textbf{The Kaplan Compute Allocation Rule}: When allocating an expanded computational budget $C \approx 6 N D$ (where 6 FLOPs are required per parameter per token during forward and backward passes), Kaplan et al. derived the following empirical power-law relationships:
    \begin{equation}
    N \propto C^{0.73}, \quad B \propto C^{0.24}, \quad S \propto C^{0.03},
    \label{eq:kaplan_power_scaling}
    \end{equation}
    where $B$ is batch size and $S$ is the number of optimization steps.
\end{enumerate}

The practical implication of Equation~\eqref{eq:kaplan_power_scaling} was profound: \emph{"As the computational budget $C$ increases, it should be spent primarily on larger models, without dramatic increases in training time or dataset size."} Specifically, a $10\times$ increase in compute budget prescribed scaling model size $N$ by $5.4\times$, but increasing training steps by merely $1.07\times$. This conclusion directly ignited the industry race toward massive models trained on relatively modest token counts.


\section{GPT-3 and In-Context Learning Dynamics}
\label{sec:gpt3_in_context_learning}

In June 2020, OpenAI published \emph{"Language Models are Few-Shot Learners"} by Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020}, introducing GPT-3. Building directly upon the Kaplan scaling laws, GPT-3 scaled model capacity to 175 billion parameters—a hundred-fold expansion over GPT-2 and an order of magnitude larger than any previously trained dense neural network.


\subsection{Model setup and architectural dimensions}
\label{subsec:gpt3_setup}

The GPT-3 architecture preserved the core decoder-only design of GPT-2, with alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the self-attention blocks to manage sequence memory:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Depth and Width}: $L = 96$ Transformer layers, each containing $H = 96$ attention heads with per-head projection dimension $d_{\text{head}} = 128$.
    \item \textbf{Hidden Dimensionality}: Hidden representation dimension $d_{\text{model}} = 12,288$, with position-wise feed-forward intermediate dimensionality $d_{\text{ff}} = 4 \times 12,288 = 49,152$.
    \item \textbf{Context Window}: Context window expanded to $L = 2048$ tokens.
    \item \textbf{Total Capacity}: $N = 175\text{B}$ parameters.
\end{itemize}

To pre-train a 175B model, OpenAI assembled a multi-source training mixture totaling 300 billion tokens. Given the variable quality of web-scraped data, corpora were filtered and sampled at differential rates:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Filtered Common Crawl} (45 TB raw $\to$ 570 GB post-filtering; 410B raw tokens): Filtered using a fastText classifier trained to discriminate high-quality WebText from uncurated web pages. During pre-training, Common Crawl was sampled at a rate of $60\%$ (180B tokens).
    \item \textbf{WebText2} (19B tokens): An expanded version of WebText, sampled at $22\%$ (66B tokens).
    \item \textbf{Books1 and Books2} (12B and 55B tokens): Literary corpora sampled at $8\%$ (24B tokens) and $8\%$ (24B tokens).
    \item \textbf{Wikipedia} (3B tokens): High-quality encyclopedic text, sampled at $3\%$ (9B tokens).
\end{enumerate}
High-quality datasets were intentionally oversampled across multiple epochs, while lower-quality scrapes were downsampled to prevent contamination.


\subsection{Fine-tuning versus in-context learning}
\label{subsec:finetuning_vs_icl}

The core pedagogical focus of the GPT-3 paper was the formalization of \emph{in-context learning} (ICL) as an alternative to supervised fine-tuning.

In the classical fine-tuning paradigm, model parameters $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ are updated using task-specific labeled datasets:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\Theta}_t - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_t; \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}).
\label{eq:finetuning_update}
\end{equation}
While effective, fine-tuning introduces substantial practical limitations: it requires curated labeled datasets for every task, risks catastrophic forgetting of general reasoning capabilities, and demands storing separate fine-tuned parameter weights for each application.

In contrast, under in-context learning, the model parameters $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ remain strictly frozen:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}.
\label{eq:frozen_parameters}
\end{equation}
The model is conditioned by supplying a natural language task description and optional demonstration exemplars directly inside the context prompt. The model then generates the target completion through next-token prediction:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y} = \arg\max_{y \in \mathcal{V}^*} P(y \mid \text{Prompt}; \boldsymbol{\Theta}),
\label{eq:icl_generation}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Prompt} = (\text{Task Instruction}, (x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_k, y_k), x_{\text{test}})$.


\subsection{The prompting spectrum: zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot}
\label{subsec:prompting_spectrum}

In-context learning operates across three primary regimes defined by the number of demonstration exemplars supplied in the context window:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Learning}: The model receives only a natural language description of the task followed by the query instance. No input-output examples are provided. This tests the model's ability to interpret task descriptions zero-shot.

    \begin{equation*}
    ((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_k, y_k)),
    \end{equation*}
    where $k$ typically ranges from 10 to 100 depending on sequence length constraints.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Empirical scaling and comparison with fine-tuned baselines}
\label{subsec:gpt3_empirical_results}

The empirical evaluation of GPT-3 revealed two fundamental insights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Emergence of Few-Shot Capability with Scale}: Smaller model variants (such as GPT-3 125M or 1.3B) exhibit near-zero performance differences between zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot regimes. However, as model capacity scales past 13B and reaches 175B, few-shot performance rises dramatically.
    \item \textbf{The Zero-to-One Shot Discontinuity}: Across nearly all evaluated tasks, the single largest performance jump occurs between the zero-shot and one-shot settings. Supplying a single input-output example provides sufficient formatting and structural alignment to guide the generation distribution.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, on open-domain question answering benchmarks (such as TriviaQA and WebQuestions), the 175B few-shot model achieved accuracies matching or outperforming fine-tuned state-of-the-art baselines (including T5-11B). This demonstrated that scaling parameter capacity allows models to acquire broad factual knowledge during pre-training, reducing the need for task-specific parameter fine-tuning.


\section{The Race to Bigger Models and the Chinchilla Scaling Revolution}
\label{sec:chinchilla_scaling}

Following the release of GPT-3 and the validation of Kaplan's compute allocation rule ($N \propto C^{0.73}$), the machine learning industry embarked on an aggressive race to train increasingly massive models. Between 2020 and 2022, leading research institutions prioritized parameter scale as the primary driver of performance.


\subsection{The escalation of model scale (2020--2022)}
\label{subsec:scale_escalation}

The post-GPT-3 period witnessed the rapid deployment of dense models well exceeding 100 billion parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Jurassic-1} (AI21 Labs, 2021): 178 billion parameters, evaluated on broad language benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{Gopher} (DeepMind, 2021): 280 billion parameters, trained on 300 billion tokens with a compute budget of $C = 5.76 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs \cite{rae2021gopher}.
    \item \textbf{Megatron-Turing NLG} (NVIDIA and Microsoft, 2021): 530 billion parameters, trained across massive distributed GPU clusters \cite{smith2022mtnlg}.
    \item \textbf{PaLM} (Google, 2022): 540 billion parameters, trained using the Pathways distributed compiler across dual TPU v4 pods \cite{chowdhery2023palm}.
\end{itemize}

During this era, foundation model publications adopted a standardized format: extensive empirical result catalogs (often exceeding 100 pages) accompanied by brief architectural descriptions. However, operational inefficiencies began to emerge: training and serving 500B+ parameter models imposed severe memory footprints and latency constraints, prompting researchers to question whether parameter expansion was being prioritized over training duration.


\subsection{The diagnosis: oversized and undertrained models}
\label{subsec:undertraining_diagnosis}

In March 2022, Hoffmann et al.~\cite{hoffmann2022} from DeepMind published \emph{"Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models"}, introducing the \textbf{Chinchilla} architecture. Their central thesis challenged the industry consensus:
\begin{quote}
\emph{"Current large language models are significantly undertrained, a consequence of the recent focus on scaling language models whilst keeping the training data constant."}
\end{quote}

Hoffmann et al. diagnosed a core methodological flaw in Kaplan's empirical setup: Kaplan et al. evaluated models using a fixed learning rate schedule that decayed learning rates across a constant step horizon, which artificially penalized models trained for longer token horizons. Furthermore, Kaplan kept training dataset sizes relatively constant ($D \approx 300\text{B}$ tokens) while expanding parameter counts ($N \to 175\text{B}$).

Re-evaluating the compute-optimal frontier, DeepMind proved that parameter count $N$ and training dataset size $D$ should be scaled in \emph{equal proportion}:
\begin{equation}
N \propto C^{0.5}, \quad D \propto C^{0.5}.
\label{eq:chinchilla_balanced_scaling}
\end{equation}
That is: \textbf{for every doubling of model parameters, the number of training tokens must also be doubled.}

To support this claim, Hoffmann et al. conducted over 400 empirical training runs across three distinct methodological approaches.


\subsection{Approach 1: Fixed-size models and training loss envelopes}
\label{subsec:chinchilla_approach1}

In Approach 1, the authors trained models spanning a broad range of fixed parameter sizes (from $70\text{M}$ to $10\text{B}$ parameters), varying the training duration and total compute allocated up to $10^{21}$ FLOPs.

\subsection{Approach 3: Parametric loss surface modeling}
\label{subsec:chinchilla_approach3}

In Approach 3, Hoffmann et al. formalized the relationship between model size, dataset size, and loss through a joint parametric formulation:
\begin{equation}
\hat{L}(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta},
\label{eq:parametric_loss_surface}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $E$ is the irreducible natural entropy of the text distribution.
    \item $A / N^\alpha$ models the empirical penalty incurred by finite model capacity.
    \item $B / D^\beta$ models the empirical penalty incurred by finite training data.
\end{itemize}

The parameters $E, A, B, \alpha, \beta$ were fitted using Huber loss minimization over all experimental runs:
\begin{equation}
E = 1.69, \quad A = 406.4, \quad B = 410.7, \quad \alpha = 0.34, \quad \beta = 0.28.
\label{eq:chinchilla_fitted_params}

To determine optimal parameter allocation under a compute budget constraint $C \approx 6 N D$, one solves the constrained optimization problem via Lagrange multipliers:
\begin{equation}
\min_{N, D} \hat{L}(N, D) \quad \text{subject to} \quad 6 N D = C.
\label{eq:lagrange_optimization}
\end{equation}
Taking partial derivatives yields the closed-form scaling solutions:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{opt}}(C) = G \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}}, \quad D_{\text{opt}}(C) = G^{-1} \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}},
\label{eq:chinchilla_closed_form}
\end{equation}
where $G = \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha + \beta}}$. Substituting the empirical parameters yields:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.45}, \quad D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.55}.
\label{eq:chinchilla_approach3_exponents}
\end{equation}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:chinchilla_approach3}, Approach 3 establishes an optimal model size between **40 and 70 billion parameters** for Gopher's budget.

While Gopher allocated its budget to maintain 280 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens, Chinchilla used a model $1/4\times$ the size (70B) trained on over $4\times$ more data (1.4 trillion tokens).

Despite having only one-fourth of Gopher's parameter count, Chinchilla uniformly outperformed Gopher, GPT-3, Jurassic-1, and Megatron-Turing NLG across an extensive suite of downstream benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding)}: Chinchilla achieved $67.5\%$ average accuracy, outperforming Gopher ($60.0\%$) and GPT-3 ($53.9\%$).
    \item \textbf{BIG-bench}: Chinchilla achieved a $10.7\%$ absolute performance improvement over Gopher.
    \item \textbf{Commonsense and Reading Comprehension}: Chinchilla outperformed GPT-3 and Gopher on LAMBADA, PIQA, and BoolQ.
\end{itemize}
The Chinchilla experiments conclusively refuted the Kaplan allocation rule, establishing that the post-GPT-3 race toward massive parameter sizes had produced oversized, undertrained architectures.


\section{The Modern Paradigm Shift and Open Foundation Models}
\label{sec:open_models_paradigm}

The Chinchilla breakthrough prompted an industry-wide transition in language model training. Rather than continuing parameter escalation ($500\text{B} \to 1\text{T}$), the field pivoted toward training compact models on massive token volumes. This shift made high-performance language models vastly more accessible, catalyzing the modern open-weight ecosystem.


\subsection{Token over-training and inference economics}
\label{subsec:inference_economics}

While Chinchilla established the \emph{compute-optimal training} ratio ($N \propto D$), real-world deployment depends heavily on \emph{inference efficiency}.

In a production environment, training compute is amortized over billions of downstream user queries. A 70B parameter model requires substantially more GPU memory bandwidth, higher latency, and complex multi-GPU tensor parallelism during inference compared to an 8B parameter model. Consequently, training a smaller model (e.g., 7B or 8B) far beyond the Chinchilla compute-optimal frontier—training on 2 trillion to 15 trillion tokens (\emph{token over-training})—incurs a higher initial training cost, but yields dramatically lower per-token latency and memory requirements at inference time.

Token over-training allowed high-performance foundation models to fit within the memory capacity of single workstation GPUs, consumer graphics cards, and edge hardware, laying the groundwork for widespread open-weight adoption.


\subsection{The LLaMA ecosystem and generational evolution}
\label{subsec:llama_family}

In February 2023, Touvron et al.~\cite{touvron2023llama} from Meta AI introduced **LLaMA** (\emph{Large Language Model Meta AI}), demonstrating that open foundation models trained exclusively on publicly available datasets could match or exceed proprietary models.

The LLaMA series evolved through successive generations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{LLaMA-1 (February 2023)}: Released in four sizes (7B, 13B, 33B, 65B) trained on 1.0T to 1.4T tokens. The 13B model outperformed GPT-3 (175B) across most benchmarks while fitting on a single consumer GPU, while LLaMA-65B rivaled Chinchilla-70B and PaLM-540B.
    
    \item \textbf{Llama 2 (July 2023)}: Trained on 2.0T tokens with an expanded context window of 4096 tokens. The architecture introduced Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) for the 70B model to reduce key-value cache memory overhead during autoregressive generation. Meta released specialized fine-tuned checkpoints, including Llama-2-Chat (aligned via Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and Code Llama (specialized for software engineering).
    
    \item \textbf{Llama 3 and 3.1 (April/July 2024)}: Scaled training data to over 15 trillion tokens with an expanded 128k context window and a 128k tiktoken vocabulary. The release featured an 8B model, a 70B model, and the flagship **405B parameter frontier model**, trained across a cluster of 16,000 H100 GPUs using 4D parallelism (tensor, pipeline, context, and data parallelism).
    
    \item \textbf{Llama 3.2 (September 2024)}: Introduced lightweight edge models (1B and 3B parameters) optimized for on-device inference, alongside multimodal vision-language models (11B and 90B parameters) capable of processing image-text inputs.
\end{enumerate}

Meta released the LLaMA weights under a permissive community license, with an explicit commercial clause requiring custom licensing only for platforms exceeding 100 million daily active users (effectively restricting competing hyperscalers while providing broad access to academic and commercial developers).


\subsection{Open foundation model families}
\label{subsec:open_model_families}

The success of the LLaMA family catalyzed a broader ecosystem of open foundation models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{EleutherAI (GPT-Neo, GPT-J, GPT-NeoX)}: An open-source research collective that championed accessible foundation models by reverse-engineering early GPT architectures and releasing **The Pile** (an 825 GB diverse text corpus).
    
    \item \textbf{Mistral AI (France)}: Founded in 2023, Mistral introduced compact, high-efficiency models utilizing Sliding Window Attention. Key releases include **Mistral 7B** (outperforming Llama 2 13B), **Mixtral 8x7B** (a sparse Mixture-of-Experts model that routes tokens dynamically through 8 feed-forward experts, activating only 2 experts per token to deliver 47B capacity with 13B active compute speed), and **Pixtral** (multimodal vision capabilities).
    
    \item \textbf{GLM (Zhipu AI and Tsinghua University, China)}: The General Language Model family, designed around an autoregressive blank-filling objective to unify bidirectional and causal modeling. GLM models emphasize bilingual proficiency across Chinese and English.
    
    \item \textbf{Falcon (Technology Innovation Institute, UAE)}: Falcon 7B, 40B, and 180B, trained on the custom-curated RefinedWeb corpus. The Falcon initiative also pioneered evaluations of non-Transformer sequence models, including state-space architectures (Mamba).
\end{itemize}
