\chapter{The Transformer architecture and attention mechanisms}
\label{ch:transformer_architecture}

\section{The architectural impasse of recurrent sequence models}
\label{sec:recurrent_impasse}

For nearly a decade, sequence modeling was dominated by Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their gated variants, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs). As established in Chapter~\ref{ch:rnn_sequence_modeling}, recurrent models represent a sequence of tokens by recursively updating an internal hidden state vector:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recurrent_transition_step}
\vh_t = f(\vh_{t-1}, \vx_t), \quad t \in \{1, 2, \dots, L\},
\end{equation}
where $\vh_t \in \R^{d_h}$ encapsulates the historical context observed up to time step $t$, and $\vx_t \in \R^d$ is the input vector representation at time $t$.

While gating mechanisms and Constant Error Carousels mitigated the vanishing gradient problem over moderate temporal spans, recurrence inherently imposes two severe, insurmountable bottlenecks that halted further scaling in language modeling:

\paragraph{1. The sequential execution wall.}
The transition equation $\vh_t = f(\vh_{t-1}, \vx_t)$ enforces an unbroken, strictly sequential causal dependency chain: computation of $\vh_t$ cannot commence until the physical memory write of $\vh_{t-1}$ is completed. Consequently, an input sequence of $L$ tokens mandates $\mathcal{O}(L)$ sequential computational operations.
Modern deep learning accelerators (GPUs and TPUs) derive their performance from massive data parallelism, executing thousands of arithmetic matrix operations concurrently across high-bandwidth tensor cores. Under recurrent processing, hardware parallelism across the temporal dimension is impossible: the accelerator is forced to idle between consecutive time steps, creating a computational wall that restricts training across massive datasets.

\paragraph{2. The information compression bottleneck of Seq2Seq encoders.}
In classical sequence-to-sequence architectures, an encoder compresses an input sequence of arbitrary length $L$ into a single, fixed-size vector $\vh_L \in \R^{d_h}$ (or a tuple $(\vh_L, \vc_L)$ in LSTMs). This single vector is subsequently handed over to the decoder to initiate target sequence generation.
As the input length $L$ expands---such as in multi-turn dialogues, technical documentation, or software engineering source code files spanning thousands of tokens---compressing all semantic, syntactic, and structural relationships into a finite-dimensional vector becomes physically impossible. Information from early time steps undergoes continuous dissipation, inducing severe contextual amnesia.

\paragraph{The architectural objective.}
These limitations raised a fundamental question: \emph{Can we design a neural architecture that completely eliminates recurrence, operates across all tokens in parallel with $\mathcal{O}(1)$ sequential operations, and allows every token to directly communicate with every other token in the sequence without a fixed-size information bottleneck?}
In 2017, this question was answered by the introduction of the \textbf{Transformer} \cite{vaswani2017}, an architecture built entirely upon multi-head attention mechanisms.

\section{The complete Transformer architecture: encoder-decoder breakdown}
\label{sec:high_level_encoder_decoder}

The foundational Transformer proposed in \cite{vaswani2017} adopts an \textbf{Encoder-Decoder} paradigm, structured to eliminate the recurrent compression bottleneck while enabling end-to-end parallel training. Figure~\ref{fig:vaswani_original_architecture} reproduces the canonical architectural diagram from the landmark paper \cite{vaswani2017}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{figures/part1/transformer_architecture_vaswani2017.png}
\caption{The original Transformer model architecture as published in \cite{vaswani2017}. The left stack corresponds to the Encoder ($N=6$ identical layers), and the right stack corresponds to the Decoder ($N=6$ identical layers), equipped with causal masked self-attention and cross-attention over the encoder representations.}
\label{fig:vaswani_original_architecture}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{1. The Encoder stack: uncompressed contextual representations.}
The Encoder consists of a stack of $N$ identical layers (typically $N=6$ in the base model). Crucially, the Encoder does \emph{not} compress the input sequence into a single summary vector. Instead, it takes the input sequence of position-augmented embeddings $\mX \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}$ and transforms it into an equally sized sequence of contextual representations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:encoder_uncompressed_output}
\mH_{\text{enc}} = [\vh_1, \vh_2, \dots, \vh_{L_{\text{enc}}}] \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}.
\end{equation}
Every output vector $\vh_i$ possesses a global receptive field that incorporates information from every other token in the sequence through bidirectional self-attention, preserving fine-grained token-level semantics without information loss.

\paragraph{2. The Decoder stack: autoregressive generation and cross-attention.}
The Decoder also consists of a stack of $N$ identical layers, operating autoregressively at inference time:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Autoregressive sequence initialization}: Generation is primed by feeding the special beginning-of-sequence delimiter $\langle\text{BOS}\rangle$ as the initial token.
    \item \textbf{Causal step-by-step prediction}: The Decoder processes the tokens generated so far, attends to the encoder representations $\mH_{\text{enc}}$, and emits logits over vocabulary $\vocab$. The highest-scoring token (or sampled token) $y_1$ is generated.
    \item \textbf{Prefix feedback loop}: The newly emitted token is appended to the decoder input, forming the context $[\langle\text{BOS}\rangle, y_1]$ to predict $y_2$. This loop repeats until the model outputs the end-of-sequence delimiter $\langle\text{EOS}\rangle$, terminating generation.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{3. The training phase: Teacher Forcing and sequence parallelization.}
At inference, the decoder must generate tokens sequentially. However, during training, the entire target sequence $[y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{L_{\text{dec}}-1}]$ is already known.
By employing \textbf{Teacher Forcing} combined with causal attention masking (which prevents tokens from attending to subsequent target tokens), all $L_{\text{dec}}$ generation steps are computed simultaneously in a single forward pass. The decoder does not unroll an iterative loop during training: the loss across all output positions is evaluated concurrently on the GPU.

\section{Attention from first principles: differentiable soft dictionary retrieval}
\label{sec:attention_first_principles}

\subsection{Why we need the attention mechanism: from RNN bottlenecks to dynamic interaction}
\label{subsec:why_we_need_attention}

To understand the conceptual necessity of the attention mechanism, we must examine the architectural bottleneck that afflicted sequential models prior to the Transformer, as formalized by Sebastian Raschka \cite{raschka2024llm}.

When translating natural language text (e.g., from German or Italian to English) or performing code generation from formal specifications, literal word-by-word substitution is fundamentally impossible. Grammatical structures, morphological agreements, and clause ordering diverge substantially between linguistic systems. Consequently, sequence transduction requires a deep model consisting of two interacting submodules: an \textbf{Encoder} that ingests and understands the source sequence, and a \textbf{Decoder} that synthesizes the target sequence.

Prior to Transformers, the dominant paradigm for sequence-to-sequence modeling was the recurrent neural network (RNN, Chapter~\ref{ch:rnn_sequence_modeling}). In an encoder-decoder RNN:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The encoder ingests source tokens $\vx_1, \vx_2, \dots, \vx_L$ sequentially, updating its recurrent hidden state at each time step via $\vh_t = f(\vh_{t-1}, \vx_t)$.
    \item At the conclusion of the source sequence, the encoder attempts to compress the entire semantic and syntactic content of the input into its final hidden state vector $\vh_L \in \R^{d_h}$.
    \item This single vector $\vh_L$ is handed over to the decoder as its initial context vector $\vc = \vh_L$, from which the decoder must generate the translated output token by token.
\end{enumerate}

This architecture suffers from a catastrophic mathematical limitation: the \textbf{information compression bottleneck} \cite{raschka2024llm}. Forcing an arbitrary-length sentence—spanning tens, hundreds, or thousands of tokens—into a single fixed-dimensional embedding vector creates an information-theoretic bottleneck. As sequence length expands:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The decoder possesses \emph{zero direct access} to intermediate representations $\vh_1, \vh_2, \dots, \vh_{L-1}$ from earlier encoder steps.
    \item Gradients backpropagating through time undergo exponential decay or explosion across recurrent chain products $\prod_{k=t+1}^L \frac{\partial \vh_k}{\partial \vh_{k-1}}$.
    \item Information from early tokens undergoes continuous dissipative dilution, causing severe context amnesia on long sequences.
\end{itemize}

To solve this dilemma, Bahdanau et al.~\cite{bahdanau2014} introduced the attention mechanism for RNNs, providing the decoder with dynamic, direct connections to \emph{all} intermediate encoder states $\vh_1, \dots, \vh_L$ at every generation step.
Three years later, Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017} realized that recurrence itself was entirely superfluous. By introducing \textbf{Self-Attention}, the model allows every position within a sequence to directly attend to and dynamically interact with every other position in the sequence across an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ sequential path length, computing rich contextual embeddings where each word's representation is dynamically updated based on the relevant context of the entire sentence \cite{raschka2024llm}.

\subsection{The associative memory analogy: queries, keys, and values}
\label{subsec:associative_memory_analogy}

To understand how attention operates mathematically from first principles, it is instructive to draw an analogy to associative retrieval structures in computer science \cite{jurafsky2024, sanderson2024attention}.

\paragraph{1. Classical dictionary lookup (discrete hash table).}
In computer systems, a dictionary or associative array stores information as a collection of $m$ discrete key-value pairs:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D} = \{ (\kappa_1, \nu_1), (\kappa_2, \nu_2), \dots, (\kappa_m, \nu_m) \}.
\end{equation}
When a discrete search query $q$ is issued, the dictionary performs an exact binary match against the keys:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discrete_lookup}
\text{\texttt{Lookup}}(q, \mathcal{D}) = \begin{cases}
\nu_k & \text{if } \exists\, k \text{ such that } q = \kappa_k, \\
\text{\texttt{None}} & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
In this classical paradigm, retrieval is binary ($0$ or $1$) and non-differentiable: if query $q$ is slightly perturbed, the lookup either matches completely or fails catastrophically.

\paragraph{2. Soft, differentiable key-value retrieval.}
Attention generalizes dictionary lookup to continuous metric vector spaces $\R^d$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{Query} vector $\vq \in \R^{d_k}$ represents the information need: \emph{``What linguistic, syntactic, or semantic attributes am I searching for?''}
    \item A set of $m$ \textbf{Key} vectors $\vk_j \in \R^{d_k}$ represent content identifiers or addresses: \emph{``What attributes do I offer?''}
    \item A set of $m$ \textbf{Value} vectors $\vv_j \in \R^{d_v}$ represent the informative content or payload: \emph{``What information do I communicate if selected?''}
\end{itemize}

Instead of checking exact equality $q = \kappa_k$, we evaluate a continuous compatibility score $s(\vq, \vk_j) \in \R$ using vector inner products:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raw_compatibility_score}
s_j = \vq^\top \vk_j.
\end{equation}
The raw alignment scores $[s_1, s_2, \dots, s_m]$ are mapped onto the probability simplex via the $\softmax$ operator, yielding non-negative attention weights that sum to unity:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:softmax_attention_weight}
\alpha_j = \frac{\exp(s_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^m \exp(s_l)}, \quad \sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j = 1, \quad \alpha_j \ge 0.
\end{equation}
Finally, the retrieved representation is computed as the \textbf{convex combination} (weighted sum) of all value vectors:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attention_convex_combination}
\vo = \sum_{j=1}^m \alpha_j \vv_j \in \R^{d_v}.
\end{equation}
Because inner products, exponentials, and summations are smooth operations, the entire lookup pipeline is fully differentiable with well-defined gradients $\frac{\partial \vo}{\partial \vq}$, $\frac{\partial \vo}{\partial \vk_j}$, and $\frac{\partial \vo}{\partial \vv_j}$.

\paragraph{Illustrative example: contextual displacement of a fluffy blue creature.}
To understand how soft key-value retrieval transforms static representations into rich contextual embeddings in high-dimensional vector spaces, consider the visual intuition formalized by Grant Sanderson \cite{sanderson2024attention} and illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:3b1b_query_key_creature}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The static embedding}: Initially, each word in the phrase \emph{``A fluffy blue creature roamed the verdant forest''} begins as an isolated unigram embedding vector $\ve \in \R^{\dmodel}$. The static vector for \emph{``creature''} merely occupies a generic semantic coordinate indicating an animal or organism, completely devoid of context regarding its visual appearance.
    \item \textbf{Queries as informational requests}: When passed into an attention block, linear projection $\mW_Q$ maps the vector for \emph{``creature''} into a Query vector $\vq_{\text{creature}}$. Geometrically, this query acts as a directional probe asking: \emph{``What descriptive adjectives precede me?''}
    \item \textbf{Keys as feature advertisements}: Concurrently, projection matrix $\mW_K$ maps all tokens into Key vectors. The preceding adjectives \emph{``fluffy''} and \emph{``blue''} produce keys $\vk_{\text{fluffy}}$ and $\vk_{\text{blue}}$ advertising: \emph{``I am a tactile descriptor''} and \emph{``I am a visual color descriptor''}.
    \item \textbf{Inner-product compatibility}: Computing inner products $\vq_{\text{creature}}^\top \vk_j$ evaluates geometric alignment (Figure~\ref{fig:3b1b_attn_scores}). Because the query explicitly seeks adjective descriptors, it achieves high positive inner products with $\vk_{\text{fluffy}}$ ($+93.0$) and $\vk_{\text{blue}}$ ($+93.4$), but low or negative alignment with irrelevant tokens like \emph{``roamed''} or \emph{``forest''}.
    \item \textbf{Value routing and displacement}: Linear projection $\mW_V$ extracts the informative payload: Value vectors $\vv_{\text{fluffy}}$ and $\vv_{\text{blue}}$ encode specific sensory features. Taking their weighted sum produces a contextual displacement vector (Figure~\ref{fig:3b1b_value_table}):
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:fluffy_blue_displacement}
    \Delta \ve_{\text{creature}} = \alpha_{\text{fluffy}} \vv_{\text{fluffy}} + \alpha_{\text{blue}} \vv_{\text{blue}}.
    \end{equation}
    When added to the initial representation via residual connection, $\ve_{\text{creature}} + \Delta \ve_{\text{creature}}$ physically moves the point in embedding space into the specialized manifold region corresponding to a \emph{fluffy, blue creature}!
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scaled dot-product attention and variance stabilization}
\label{sec:scaled_dot_product_attention}

\subsection{Definition of the scaled attention mechanism}
\label{subsec:scaled_dot_product_defn}

When computing alignment scores between queries and keys, standard dot product $s = \vq^\top \vk$ suffers from a critical numerical failure mode as vector dimensionality increases. To resolve this, the landmark formulation in \cite{vaswani2017} introduced \textbf{Scaled Dot-Product Attention}.

\begin{definition}{Scaled dot-product attention}{scaled_dot_product_defn}
Let $\mQ \in \R^{L_Q \times d_k}$, $\mK \in \R^{L_K \times d_k}$, and $\mV \in \R^{L_K \times d_v}$ denote Query, Key, and Value matrices. The Scaled Dot-Product Attention operator evaluates:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scaled_dot_product_matrix}
\text{\texttt{Attention}}(\mQ, \mK, \mV) = \softmax\left( \frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \mM \right) \mV,
\end{equation}
where $\mM \in \{0, -\infty\}^{L_Q \times L_K}$ is an optional masking matrix, and $\softmax$ is applied row-wise.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Why linear projections are mandatory}
\label{subsec:why_linear_projections}

In self-attention, input tokens start as embeddings $\vx_i \in \R^{\dmodel}$. If we computed dot products directly between raw token embeddings $\vx_i^\top \vx_j$, two critical limitations would cripple model expressivity:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetry trap}: Because the dot product is commutative ($\vx_i^\top \vx_j = \vx_j^\top \vx_i$), the unprojected affinity between token $i$ and token $j$ would be strictly symmetric. A token could not express an asymmetric relationship, such as a verb actively seeking its direct object without the direct object reciprocating with identical strength.
    \item \textbf{Role collapse}: A token vector would be forced to play three fundamentally distinct roles simultaneously: formulating a search request (Query), advertising its semantic contents (Key), and providing the contextual payload (Value).
\end{enumerate}
To break this symmetry and enable functional specialization, the model projects each input vector into three distinct latent subspaces using learned weight matrices:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qkv_linear_projections}
\mQ = \mX \mW_Q, \quad \mK = \mX \mW_K, \quad \mV = \mX \mW_V,
\end{equation}
where $\mW_Q \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_k}$, $\mW_K \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_k}$, and $\mW_V \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_v}$.

\subsection{Complete numerical walkthrough: reproducing the lecture calculations}
\label{subsec:complete_numerical_walkthrough}

To illustrate these matrix projections and attention operations concretely, we walk through step-by-step the complete numerical worked example, tracking intermediate values down to four decimal places.

\paragraph{1. Initial setup.}
Consider an input sequence of $L = 3$ tokens in a 2-dimensional latent embedding space ($\dmodel = 2$), with projection dimensions $d_k = d_v = 2$:
\begin{equation}
\mX = \begin{bmatrix}
0.1 & 0.5 \\
0.1 & 0.2 \\
0.9 & 0.9
\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{3 \times 2}, \quad
\mW_Q = \begin{bmatrix}
0.9 & 0.9 \\
0.5 & 0.3
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mW_K = \begin{bmatrix}
1.0 & 0.9 \\
1.0 & 0.6
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mW_V = \begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & 0.9 \\
0.9 & 1.0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{2. Linear projection into Queries, Keys, and Values.}
Multiplying $\mX$ by each projection matrix yields:
\begin{equation}
\mQ = \mX \mW_Q = \begin{bmatrix}
0.1(0.9) + 0.5(0.5) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.5(0.3) \\
0.1(0.9) + 0.2(0.5) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.2(0.3) \\
0.9(0.9) + 0.9(0.5) & 0.9(0.9) + 0.9(0.3)
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
0.34 & 0.24 \\
0.19 & 0.15 \\
1.26 & 1.08
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mK = \mX \mW_K = \begin{bmatrix}
0.1(1.0) + 0.5(1.0) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.5(0.6) \\
0.1(1.0) + 0.2(1.0) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.2(0.6) \\
0.9(1.0) + 0.9(1.0) & 0.9(0.9) + 0.9(0.6)
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
0.60 & 0.39 \\
0.30 & 0.21 \\
1.80 & 1.35
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mV = \mX \mW_V = \begin{bmatrix}
0.1(0.8) + 0.5(0.9) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.5(1.0) \\
0.1(0.8) + 0.2(0.9) & 0.1(0.9) + 0.2(1.0) \\
0.9(0.8) + 0.9(0.9) & 0.9(0.9) + 0.9(1.0)
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
0.53 & 0.59 \\
0.26 & 0.29 \\
1.53 & 1.71
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{3. Directly computing raw dot products $\mQ\mK^\top$ and scaled logits $\mS = \frac{\mQ\mK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}$.}
Evaluating the full matrix multiplication $\mQ \mK^\top \in \R^{3 \times 3}$ directly:
\begin{equation}
\mQ \mK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
0.34 & 0.24 \\
0.19 & 0.15 \\
1.26 & 1.08
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0.60 & 0.30 & 1.80 \\
0.39 & 0.21 & 1.35
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
0.2976 & 0.1524 & 0.9360 \\
0.1725 & 0.0885 & 0.5445 \\
1.1772 & 0.6048 & 3.7260
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Alongside this matrix computation, examining row 1 reveals the individual scalar dot-product compatibility scores for the first token ($\vq_1 = [0.34, 0.24]$):
\begin{align}
s_{1,1} &= \vq_1^\top \vk_1 = 0.34(0.60) + 0.24(0.39) = 0.2040 + 0.0936 = 0.2976, \\
s_{1,2} &= \vq_1^\top \vk_2 = 0.34(0.30) + 0.24(0.21) = 0.1020 + 0.0504 = 0.1524, \\
s_{1,3} &= \vq_1^\top \vk_3 = 0.34(1.80) + 0.24(1.35) = 0.6120 + 0.3240 = 0.9360.
\end{align}
Next, we scale the complete matrix by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:matrix_scaled_scores_numerical}
\mS = \frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{2}} = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{0.2976}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{0.1524}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{0.9360}{\sqrt{2}} \\[4pt]
\frac{0.1725}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{0.0885}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{0.5445}{\sqrt{2}} \\[4pt]
\frac{1.1772}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{0.6048}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{3.7260}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{bmatrix}
\approx \begin{bmatrix}
0.2104 & 0.1078 & 0.6619 \\
0.1220 & 0.0626 & 0.3850 \\
0.8324 & 0.4277 & 2.6347
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{4. Matrix softmax normalization onto the probability simplex.}
Applying the row-wise softmax operator $\mA = \softmax(\mS)$ exponentiates each row and normalizes by its partition sum.
For row 1:
\begin{align}
\exp(0.2104) &\approx 1.2342, \quad \exp(0.1078) \approx 1.1138, \quad \exp(0.6619) \approx 1.9385, \\
\sum_{j=1}^3 \exp(\tilde{s}_{1,j}) &= 1.2342 + 1.1138 + 1.9385 = 4.2865, \\
\alpha_{1,1} &= \frac{1.2342}{4.2865} \approx 0.2879, \quad \alpha_{1,2} = \frac{1.1138}{4.2865} \approx 0.2598, \quad \alpha_{1,3} = \frac{1.9385}{4.2865} \approx 0.4522.
\end{align}
Evaluating rows 2 and 3 identically yields the full row-stochastic attention weight matrix:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:matrix_attention_weights_numerical}
\mA = \softmax\left(\frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}
0.2879 & 0.2598 & 0.4522 \\
0.3083 & 0.2906 & 0.4011 \\
0.1294 & 0.0863 & 0.7843
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where each row sums precisely to $1.0000$.

\paragraph{5. Matrix value aggregation: computing the full output matrix $\mO = \mA \mV$.}

\end{equation}

\subsection{Dimensional constraints across projection matrices}
\label{subsec:dimensional_constraints}

The dimensional compatibility of the projection matrices is governed by three fundamental algebraic constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Queries and Keys must share identical dimensionality ($d_k$)}: The dot product $\vq^\top \vk$ is an inner product between two vectors. An inner product is mathematically undefined unless both vectors live in the identical Euclidean vector space $\R^{d_k}$. Therefore, $\mW_Q$ and $\mW_K$ must always share identical column dimension $d_k$.
    \item \textbf{Values can possess independent dimensionality ($d_v$)}: Value vectors are never multiplied with queries or keys. They are only scaled by scalar attention weights $\alpha_j \in [0, 1]$ and summed together. Consequently, $d_v$ could mathematically differ from $d_k$. In standard architectures (including \cite{vaswani2017}), practitioners choose $d_k = d_v = \dmodel / h$ purely for architectural symmetry and implementation simplicity.
    \item \textbf{Sequence lengths in self-attention vs. cross-attention}: In self-attention, $L_Q = L_K = L_V = L$. In cross-attention, queries stem from the decoder ($L_Q = L_{\text{dec}}$), while keys and values stem from the encoder ($L_K = L_V = L_{\text{enc}}$). The dot-product matrix $\mQ \mK^\top$ has shape $L_{\text{dec}} \times L_{\text{enc}}$, and multiplying by $\mV \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times d_v}$ yields the required decoder output shape $L_{\text{dec}} \times d_v$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mathematical derivation: theoretical necessity of the \texorpdfstring{$\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$}{1/sqrt(dk)} scaling factor}
\label{subsec:scaling_factor_derivation}

Why must the dot product $\vq^\top \vk$ be scaled specifically by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$? To understand the mathematical necessity of this normalization, consider the variance behavior of unscaled dot products as dimensionality expands.

\paragraph{Proof of dot-product variance expansion.}
Assume that the components of query vector $\vq \in \R^{d_k}$ and key vector $\vk \in \R^{d_k}$ are independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with zero mean and unit variance:
\begin{equation}
\E[q_i] = 0, \quad \Var(q_i) = 1, \quad \E[k_i] = 0, \quad \Var(k_i) = 1, \quad \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, d_k\}.
\end{equation}
The unscaled dot product is the random variable $z = \vq^\top \vk = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$.
By linearity of expectation and independence:
\begin{equation}
\E[z] = \E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \E[q_i]\E[k_i] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} (0 \cdot 0) = 0.
\end{equation}
To compute the variance of $z$, we expand the definition $\Var(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \Var(q_i k_i)$. Using identity $\Var(X Y) = \E[X^2]\E[Y^2] - (\E[X]\E[Y])^2$:
\begin{align}
\Var(q_i k_i) &= \E[q_i^2]\E[k_i^2] - (\E[q_i]\E[k_i])^2 \nonumber \\
&= \left(\Var(q_i) + (\E[q_i])^2\right)\left(\Var(k_i) + (\E[k_i])^2\right) - 0 \nonumber \\
&= (1 + 0)(1 + 0) = 1.
\end{align}
Summing over all $d_k$ independent dimensions:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:variance_unscaled_dot_product}
\Var(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \Var(q_i k_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} 1 = d_k.
\end{equation}
Thus, the standard deviation of the dot product is $\sigma_z = \sqrt{d_k}$.

\paragraph{The gradient saturation consequence.}
For large dimensions (e.g., $d_k = 64$ or $128$), the variance $d_k$ is substantial, causing dot products to take values with magnitude $|z| \gg 1$.
Recall that the derivative of the softmax function with respect to logit $z_i$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:softmax_derivative}
\frac{\partial \softmax(z)_i}{\partial z_j} = \softmax(z)_i \left(\delta_{ij} - \softmax(z)_j\right).
\end{equation}
When logits $z$ have large absolute variance, the exponential $\exp(z_m)$ of the maximal logit completely dominates the sum. The softmax output degenerates into a nearly one-hot indicator distribution ($\alpha_m \approx 1$, and $\alpha_{j \ne m} \approx 0$). In this regime:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \softmax(z)_i}{\partial z_j} \approx 0, \quad \forall i, j.
\end{equation}
The gradients vanish, arresting backpropagation through the attention module.
By scaling $z$ by scalar factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$, we renormalize the variance back to unity:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:variance_scaled_dot_product}
\Var\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{1}{(\sqrt{d_k})^2}\Var(z) = \frac{1}{d_k} \cdot d_k = 1.
\end{equation}
Renormalizing the variance preserves healthy gradient dynamics across arbitrarily deep networks.

\subsection{Encoder self-attention: unconstrained bidirectional contextualization}
\label{subsec:encoder_self_attention}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[anchor=south west, inner sep=0] (image) at (0,0) {\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/part1/transformer_architecture_vaswani2017.png}};

        \node[font=\footnotesize\sffamily\bfseries, fill=crimsonRed, text=white, rounded corners=2pt, inner sep=2pt] at (0.3225, 0.372) {Encoder Multi-Head Attention};
    \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Location of Encoder Self-Attention within the canonical Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017}. The module resides in the left encoder stack, processing input token representations with full unconstrained bidirectional visibility.}
\label{fig:vaswani_highlight_encoder_mha}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why unconstrained bidirectional visibility is mandatory.}
In natural language understanding, the meaning of a word is profoundly shaped by tokens that appear both \emph{before} and \emph{after} it. For example, consider syntactic ambiguity, pronoun reference, or polysemy: in the sentence \emph{``The bank of the river overflowed''}, the polysemous noun \emph{``bank''} cannot be resolved into a geological landform rather than a financial institution without inspecting the downstream word \emph{``river''}. 
Because the Encoder's sole objective is representation learning rather than autoregressive word generation, there is no risk of label leakage. Therefore, the Encoder must have access to the entire sentence simultaneously: the attention matrix $\mA \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times L_{\text{enc}}}$ is completely unconstrained ($\mM = \mat{0}$), granting every token an instantaneous $\mathcal{O}(1)$ global receptive field across all $L_{\text{enc}}$ positions.

In the Encoder, queries, keys, and values are all linearly projected from the preceding encoder layer's representations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:encoder_self_attn_projections}
\mQ = \mX \mW_Q, \quad \mK = \mX \mW_K, \quad \mV = \mX \mW_V,
\end{equation}
where $\mX \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}$, $\mW_Q, \mW_K \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_k}$, and $\mW_V \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_v}$. No masking matrix is applied ($\mM = \mat{0}$). Every token $i$ can attend freely to all other tokens $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, L_{\text{enc}}\}$, producing unconstrained bidirectional representations.

\paragraph{Step-by-step mathematical example: bidirectional polysemy disambiguation.}
Consider an English input sentence of $L_{\text{enc}} = 3$ tokens:
\begin{equation}
X = [\text{``bank''}_1,\, \text{``of''}_2,\, \text{``river''}_3].
\end{equation}
The token \emph{``bank''} is polysemous. Without inspecting the future token \emph{``river''}, disambiguation is impossible.
Let $d_k = d_v = 2$. Suppose the linear projections $\mX \mW_Q$, $\mX \mW_K$, and $\mX \mW_V$ yield:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:enc_example_matrices}
\mQ = \begin{bmatrix}
1.0 & 0.5 \\
0.2 & 0.8 \\
0.9 & 1.2
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mK = \begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & 0.4 \\
0.1 & 0.9 \\
1.0 & 1.1
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mV = \begin{bmatrix}
1.5 & 0.2 \\
0.1 & 0.8 \\
0.4 & 2.0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We execute the attention mechanism step by step:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Raw dot-product computation}:
    Evaluating unscaled inner products $\mQ \mK^\top \in \R^{3 \times 3}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \mQ \mK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
    (1.0)(0.8) + (0.5)(0.4) & (1.0)(0.1) + (0.5)(0.9) & (1.0)(1.0) + (0.5)(1.1) \\
    (0.2)(0.8) + (0.8)(0.4) & (0.2)(0.1) + (0.8)(0.9) & (0.2)(1.0) + (0.8)(1.1) \\
    (0.9)(0.8) + (1.2)(0.4) & (0.9)(0.1) + (1.2)(0.9) & (0.9)(1.0) + (1.2)(1.1)
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    Evaluating each scalar entry yields:
    \begin{equation}
    \mQ \mK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
    1.00 & 0.55 & 1.55 \\
    0.48 & 0.74 & 1.08 \\
    1.20 & 1.17 & 2.22
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Scaling by $\sqrt{d_k} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:enc_scaled_scores}
    \mS = \frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.707 & 0.389 & 1.096 \\
    0.339 & 0.523 & 0.764 \\
    0.849 & 0.827 & 1.570
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional softmax normalization}:
    Since no mask is present ($\mM = \mat{0}$), we compute row-wise $\softmax$ directly across all columns:
    For row 1 (corresponding to \emph{``bank''}):
    \begin{align}
    \exp(0.707) &\approx 2.028, \quad \exp(0.389) \approx 1.475, \quad \exp(1.096) \approx 2.992, \\
    Z_1 &= 2.028 + 1.475 + 2.992 = 6.495, \\
    \alpha_{11} &= \frac{2.028}{6.495} \approx 0.312, \quad
    \alpha_{12} = \frac{1.475}{6.495} \approx 0.227, \quad
    \alpha_{13} = \frac{2.992}{6.495} \approx 0.461.
    \end{align}
    Applying this across all rows yields the dense attention matrix:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:enc_attn_weights}
    \mA_{\text{enc}} = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.312 & 0.227 & 0.461 \\
    0.268 & 0.322 & 0.410 \\
    0.248 & 0.243 & 0.510
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Contextual value aggregation}:
    Multiplying $\mA_{\text{enc}}$ by $\mV \in \R^{3 \times 2}$ yields the updated contextual representations:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:enc_output_matrix}
    \mO_{\text{enc}} = \mA_{\text{enc}} \mV = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.312 \vv_1 + 0.227 \vv_2 + 0.461 \vv_3 \\
    0.268 \vv_1 + 0.322 \vv_2 + 0.410 \vv_3 \\
    0.248 \vv_1 + 0.243 \vv_2 + 0.510 \vv_3
    \end{bmatrix}
    \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.675 & 1.165 \\
    0.598 & 1.131 \\
    0.600 & 1.263
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Key architectural takeaway.}
Notice that token 1 (\emph{``bank''}) allocates its largest attention weight ($\alpha_{13} = 0.461$) to token 3 (\emph{``river''}), looking \emph{ahead} into future context. This unconstrained bidirectional information exchange allows \emph{``bank''} to instantly absorb the geological semantics of \emph{``river''} into its contextual output $\vo_1$.

\subsection{Decoder masked self-attention: causal autoregressive constraint}
\label{subsec:decoder_masked_self_attention}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[anchor=south west, inner sep=0] (image) at (0,0) {\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/part1/transformer_architecture_vaswani2017.png}};
    \begin{scope}[x={(image.south east)}, y={(image.north west)}]
        \draw[examAmber, very thick, rounded corners=3pt, fill=examAmber, fill opacity=0.18] (0.562, 0.342) rectangle (0.793, 0.428);
        \node[font=\footnotesize\sffamily\bfseries, fill=examAmber, text=white, rounded corners=2pt, inner sep=2pt] at (0.6775, 0.385) {Masked Multi-Head Attention};
    \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Location of Masked Multi-Head Attention within the canonical Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017}. The module resides at the base of the right decoder stack, applying causal masking to enforce strictly autoregressive sequence generation.}
\label{fig:vaswani_highlight_masked_mha}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the causal mask is mandatory for autoregressive decoding.}
Unlike the Encoder, the Decoder is tasked with sequential language generation: producing the next token $y_t$ conditioned strictly on the prefix of tokens emitted so far $[y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{t-1}]$. At test time, future tokens $y_{t+1}, y_{t+2}, \dots$ do not exist.
During training, however, the entire target sequence is supplied to the model simultaneously to enable parallel execution via Teacher Forcing. If unconstrained self-attention were permitted in the decoder, a token at position $t$ could directly attend to position $t+1$ and cheat by copying the ground-truth answer. The model would suffer from catastrophic information leakage, failing to learn any predictive distribution over future tokens.
To enforce the arrow of time while preserving parallel tensor execution on hardware, a \textbf{causal mask} $\mM$ is added to the attention logits prior to softmax, strictly setting all future attention weights to zero.

To preserve causality while retaining parallel training under Teacher Forcing, a \textbf{causal mask} $\mM \in \R^{L_{\text{dec}} \times L_{\text{dec}}}$ is added to the scaled dot products prior to the softmax operator:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:causal_mask_definition}
M_{i, j} = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } j \le i, \\
-\infty & \text{if } j > i.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
In matrix form for a sequence of length 3:
\begin{equation}
\mM = \begin{bmatrix}
0 & -\infty & -\infty \\
0 & 0 & -\infty \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Because $\exp(-\infty) = 0$, evaluating the row-wise softmax guarantees:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{i, j} = 0 \quad \forall j > i.
\end{equation}
From a hardware efficiency standpoint, computing the full dense GEMM operation $\mQ \mK^\top$ followed by a tensor addition with $\mM$ fully saturates GPU tensor cores and memory buses, outperforming jagged triangular loops.

\paragraph{Step-by-step mathematical example: autoregressive generation under causal masking.}
Now consider the Decoder generating the Italian translation prefix of length $L_{\text{dec}} = 3$:
\begin{equation}
Y = [y_1 = \langle\text{BOS}\rangle,\, y_2 = \text{``riva''},\, y_3 = \text{``del''}].
\end{equation}
All queries, keys, and values are projected from the decoder's target token embeddings ($d_k = d_v = 2$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dec_example_matrices}
\mQ = \begin{bmatrix}
0.5 & 0.5 \\
1.2 & 0.3 \\
0.8 & 1.0
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mK = \begin{bmatrix}
0.6 & 0.4 \\
1.0 & 0.2 \\
0.7 & 0.9
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mV = \begin{bmatrix}
0.2 & 0.5 \\
1.8 & 0.4 \\
0.6 & 1.4
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let us trace the computation step by step:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Raw scaled dot products (unmasked)}:
    Computing $\mS = \frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{2}}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dec_unmasked_scores}
    \mS = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}
    0.50 & 0.60 & 0.80 \\
    0.84 & 1.26 & 1.11 \\
    0.88 & 1.00 & 1.46
    \end{bmatrix}
    \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.354 & 0.424 & 0.566 \\
    0.594 & 0.891 & 0.785 \\
    0.622 & 0.707 & 1.032
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    Notice that unmasked score $S_{13} = 0.566$ is the highest in the first row: without masking, $\langle\text{BOS}\rangle$ would strongly attend to future token \emph{``del''}!
    \item \textbf{Applying the causal mask $\mM$}:
    We add $\mM$ to $\mS$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dec_masked_scores}
    \mS_{\text{masked}} = \mS + \mM = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.354 & -\infty & -\infty \\
    0.594 & 0.891 & -\infty \\
    0.622 & 0.707 & 1.032
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Row-wise softmax under masking}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Row 1 ($y_1 = \langle\text{BOS}\rangle$)}:
        $\exp(0.354) \approx 1.425$, while $\exp(-\infty) = 0$:
        \begin{equation}
        \alpha_{11} = \frac{1.425}{1.425 + 0 + 0} = 1.000, \quad \alpha_{12} = 0.000, \quad \alpha_{13} = 0.000.
        \end{equation}
        Position 1 can \emph{only} attend to itself!
        \item \textbf{Row 2 ($y_2 = \text{``riva''}$)}:
        $\exp(0.594) \approx 1.811$, $\exp(0.891) \approx 2.438$, $\exp(-\infty) = 0$:
        \begin{align}
        Z_2 &= 1.811 + 2.438 = 4.249, \\
        \alpha_{21} &= \frac{1.811}{4.249} \approx 0.426, \quad
        \alpha_{22} = \frac{2.438}{4.249} \approx 0.574, \quad
        \alpha_{23} = 0.000.
        \end{align}
        Position 2 attends strictly to the prefix $\{y_1, y_2\}$; future token $y_3$ is zeroed out.
        \item \textbf{Row 3 ($y_3 = \text{``del''}$)}:
        $\exp(0.622) \approx 1.863$, $\exp(0.707) \approx 2.028$, $\exp(1.032) \approx 2.807$:
        \begin{equation}
        Z_3 = 1.863 + 2.028 + 2.807 = 6.698 \implies
        \alpha_{31} \approx 0.278, \quad \alpha_{32} \approx 0.303, \quad \alpha_{33} \approx 0.419.
        \end{equation}
    \end{itemize}
    This produces the strictly lower-triangular attention matrix:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dec_causal_weights}
    \mA_{\text{dec}} = \begin{bmatrix}
    1.000 & 0.000 & 0.000 \\
    0.426 & 0.574 & 0.000 \\
    0.278 & 0.303 & 0.419
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Causal value aggregation}:
    Multiplying by $\mV \in \R^{3 \times 2}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dec_output_matrix}
    \mO_{\text{dec}} = \mA_{\text{dec}} \mV = \begin{bmatrix}
    1.000 \vv_1 \\
    0.426 \vv_1 + 0.574 \vv_2 \\
    0.278 \vv_1 + 0.303 \vv_2 + 0.419 \vv_3
    \end{bmatrix}
    \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.200 & 0.500 \\
    1.118 & 0.443 \\
    0.852 & 0.847
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Key architectural takeaway.}
Contrast this directly with Encoder self-attention: despite $S_{13} = 0.566$ having high compatibility, the causal mask completely extinguishes its influence ($\alpha_{13} = 0$). Output $\vo_1$ is computed strictly from $\vv_1$, preserving the causality required for autoregressive sequence modeling.

\subsection{Encoder-decoder cross-attention: dynamic source grounding}
\label{subsec:cross_attention_mechanics}

\caption{Location of Encoder-Decoder Cross-Attention within the canonical Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017}. The module bridges the two stacks: queries $\mathbf{Q}$ are generated by the decoder, while keys $\mathbf{K}$ and values $\mathbf{V}$ are supplied directly from the output of the encoder stack.}
\label{fig:vaswani_highlight_cross_mha}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why queries originate from the decoder while keys and values originate from the encoder.}
In sequence-to-sequence translation, the decoder is generating tokens in the target language, while the encoder holds the semantic context of the source sentence.
Recall the fundamental roles of the attention triad:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{Query} asks: \emph{``What information am I currently searching for to generate my next word?''} Because the entity generating the next word is the decoder, the queries $\mQ$ must be projected from the decoder's current representation.
    \item A \textbf{Key} advertises: \emph{``What features do I describe in the source sentence?''}
    \item A \textbf{Value} provides: \emph{``What source meaning should be retrieved?''}
\end{itemize}
Because the source content is held exclusively in the encoder, the keys $\mK$ and values $\mV$ must originate from the encoder's output representations. If queries were taken from the encoder instead, the source sentence would be searching the target words, which completely contradicts the directional objective of translating source meaning into target text. By projecting queries from the decoder and keys/values from the encoder, cross-attention acts as a dynamic search engine allowing each generated target token to query the entire uncompressed source sentence.

In cross-attention, the model bridges the target sequence with the source sequence.
The queries originate from the decoder's intermediate representations $\mH_{\text{dec}} \in \R^{L_{\text{dec}} \times \dmodel}$, while keys and values originate from the uncompressed output of the encoder stack $\mH_{\text{enc}} \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_attention_projections_explicit}
\mQ = \mH_{\text{dec}} \mW_Q^{\text{cross}} \in \R^{L_{\text{dec}} \times d_k}, \quad
\mK = \mH_{\text{enc}} \mW_K^{\text{cross}} \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times d_k}, \quad
\mV = \mH_{\text{enc}} \mW_V^{\text{cross}} \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times d_v}.
\end{equation}
Crucially:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric sequence lengths}: The number of queries $L_{\text{dec}}$ does not need to equal the number of keys/values $L_{\text{enc}}$. In translation, sentences in different languages rarely have identical token counts.
    \item \textbf{No causal mask on the source}: The source sentence was fully processed by the encoder before decoding began. Thus, every decoder query position can inspect \emph{all} $L_{\text{enc}}$ source tokens ($\mM = \mat{0}$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Step-by-step mathematical example: cross-lingual alignment with asymmetric lengths.}
Suppose our decoder has generated a target prefix of length $L_{\text{dec}} = 2$ ($[\langle\text{BOS}\rangle,\, \text{``riva''}]$), and cross-attends to our earlier English source sentence of length $L_{\text{enc}} = 3$ ($[\text{``bank''},\, \text{``of''},\, \text{``river''}]$).
The decoder provides $L_{\text{dec}} = 2$ queries $\mQ \in \R^{2 \times 2}$ (taken from the first two rows of \eqref{eq:dec_example_matrices}), while the encoder provides $L_{\text{enc}} = 3$ keys and values $\mK, \mV \in \R^{3 \times 2}$ (from \eqref{eq:enc_example_matrices}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_example_matrices}
\mQ = \begin{bmatrix}
0.5 & 0.5 \\
1.2 & 0.3
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mK = \begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & 0.4 \\
0.1 & 0.9 \\
1.0 & 1.1
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mV = \begin{bmatrix}
1.5 & 0.2 \\
0.1 & 0.8 \\
0.4 & 2.0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let us trace the rectangular cross-attention computation step by step:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rectangular dot-product matrix ($L_{\text{dec}} \times L_{\text{enc}} = 2 \times 3$)}:
    \begin{equation}
    \mQ \mK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
    (0.5)(0.8) + (0.5)(0.4) & (0.5)(0.1) + (0.5)(0.9) & (0.5)(1.0) + (0.5)(1.1) \\
    (1.2)(0.8) + (0.3)(0.4) & (1.2)(0.1) + (0.3)(0.9) & (1.2)(1.0) + (0.3)(1.1)
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    Evaluating the pairwise inner products yields:
    \begin{equation}
    \mQ \mK^\top = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.60 & 0.50 & 1.05 \\
    1.08 & 0.39 & 1.53
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Scaling by $\sqrt{d_k} = \sqrt{2}$}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cross_scaled_scores}
    \mS_{\text{cross}} = \frac{\mQ \mK^\top}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.424 & 0.354 & 0.742 \\
    0.764 & 0.276 & 1.082
    \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{2 \times 3}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Row-wise softmax normalization (over $L_{\text{enc}} = 3$ source keys)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Row 1 ($\vq_{\langle\text{BOS}\rangle}$)}:
        $\exp(0.424) \approx 1.528$, $\exp(0.354) \approx 1.425$, $\exp(0.742) \approx 2.100$:
        \begin{equation}
        Z_1 = 1.528 + 1.425 + 2.100 = 5.053 \implies
        \mA_{1, :} \approx [0.302, \; 0.282, \; 0.416].
        \end{equation}
        \item \textbf{Row 2 ($\vq_{\text{``riva''}}$)}:
        $\exp(0.764) \approx 2.147$, $\exp(0.276) \approx 1.318$, $\exp(1.082) \approx 2.951$:
        \begin{equation}
        Z_2 = 2.147 + 1.318 + 2.951 = 6.416 \implies
        \mA_{2, :} \approx [0.335, \; 0.205, \; 0.460].
        \end{equation}
    \end{itemize}
    This yields the rectangular cross-attention matrix:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cross_attn_weights}
    \mA_{\text{cross}} = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.302 & 0.282 & 0.416 \\
    0.335 & 0.205 & 0.460
    \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{2 \times 3}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Cross-attention context retrieval}:
    Multiplying $\mA_{\text{cross}} \in \R^{2 \times 3}$ by encoder values $\mV \in \R^{3 \times 2}$ yields the grounded representations $\mO_{\text{cross}} \in \R^{2 \times 2}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cross_output_matrix}
    \mO_{\text{cross}} = \mA_{\text{cross}} \mV = \begin{bmatrix}
    0.302 \vv_1 + 0.282 \vv_2 + 0.416 \vv_3 \\
    0.335 \vv_1 + 0.205 \vv_2 + 0.460 \vv_3
    \end{bmatrix}
    \approx \begin{bmatrix}
    0.648 & 1.117 \\
    0.706 & 1.151
    \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Key architectural takeaway.}
Query $\vq_{\text{``riva''}}$ strongly aligns with source key $\vk_{\text{``river''}}$ ($\alpha_{23} = 0.460$) and source key $\vk_{\text{``bank''}}$ ($\alpha_{21} = 0.335$). The decoder directly pulls the exact semantic coordinates of the geological shore from the source representation without compressing the source into a single bottleneck vector!

\section{Multi-head attention (MHA)}
\label{sec:multi_head_attention}

\paragraph{The limitation of single-head attention.}
In single-head attention, the model computes a single set of attention weights $\alpha_{i, j}$, averaging all values into a single weighted sum. However, a single token often participates in multiple simultaneous linguistic relationships:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A verb must track both its grammatical subject (often located earlier in the sentence) and its direct object (often located downstream).
    \item In programming languages, an identifier must link to its type declaration, its scope boundary, and its mutating assignments.
\end{itemize}
A single attention head cannot focus simultaneously on disparate positions with different semantic intents: the softmax distribution is forced to average across them, diluting specific relational signals.

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
Multi-Head Attention (MHA) resolves this by linearly projecting queries, keys, and values $h$ times with different, learned parameter matrices:
\begin{definition}{Multi-head attention}{mha_defn}
Let $h$ denote the number of attention heads. Multi-Head Attention projects $\mQ, \mK, \mV$ into $h$ distinct representation subspaces of dimension $d_k = \dmodel / h$ and $d_v = \dmodel / h$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mha_formula}
\text{\texttt{MultiHead}}(\mQ, \mK, \mV) = \left[ \text{head}_1 \parallel \text{head}_2 \parallel \dots \parallel \text{head}_h \right] \mW^O,
\end{equation}
where each individual head is evaluated via:
\begin{equation}
\text{head}_i = \text{\texttt{Attention}}(\mQ \mW_i^Q, \mK \mW_i^K, \mV \mW_i^V), \quad i \in \{1, 2, \dots, h\},
\end{equation}
with parameter projection matrices $\mW_i^Q \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_k}$, $\mW_i^K \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_k}$, $\mW_i^V \in \R^{\dmodel \times d_v}$, and output projection matrix $\mW^O \in \R^{h d_v \times \dmodel}$.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Parameter equivalence and computational efficiency.}
Due to the reduced dimension of each head ($d_k = d_v = \dmodel / h$), the total computational cost of multi-head attention is identical to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality $\dmodel$:
\begin{equation}
h \times \left( \dmodel \times \frac{\dmodel}{h} \right) = \dmodel^2 \text{ operations for projections}.
\end{equation}
In standard configurations (e.g., $\dmodel = 512, h = 8 \implies d_k = d_v = 64$, or $\dmodel = 768, h = 12 \implies d_k = d_v = 64$), parallel heads learn specialized sub-behaviors: some heads attend to local syntactic structure, others to long-range dependencies, and others to delimiter tokens.

\section{Sublayer mechanics: residual connections and Layer Normalization}
\label{sec:sublayer_mechanics}

In deep Transformer architectures comprising dozens of layers, stacking attention blocks and non-linear feed-forward networks directly would lead to optimization failure due to exploding or vanishing gradients. To ensure stable gradient propagation, each Transformer sublayer is wrapped in a residual connection followed by Layer Normalization:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:add_and_norm_post}
\vy = \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}(\vx + \text{\texttt{Sublayer}}(\vx)).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{1. Residual skip connections (gradient superhighways).}
First introduced in \cite{he2016deep} for deep residual networks (ResNets), identity shortcut connections pass the unmodified input $\vx$ around the sublayer (Figure~\ref{fig:slide75_residual_connections}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_connection}
\vy = \vx + \mathcal{F}(\vx),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}(\vx)$ represents the multi-head attention or feed-forward transformation.

During backpropagation, the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to input $\vx$ expands via the chain rule to:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residual_gradient_flow}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vx} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vy} \frac{\partial \vy}{\partial \vx} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vy} \left( \mI + \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}(\vx)}{\partial \vx} \right) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vy} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vy} \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}(\vx)}{\partial \vx}.
\end{equation}
The presence of the identity matrix $\mI$ guarantees an unobstructed \textbf{gradient superhighway}: even if the Jacobian $\frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \vx}$ vanishes (for instance, when weights are initialized small or activations saturate), term $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vy}$ flows directly back to earlier layers in a single step, completely preventing gradient extinction.

\begin{definition}{Layer Normalization}{layer_norm_defn}
For a token representation $\vx = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d]^\top \in \R^d$, Layer Normalization evaluates:
\begin{align}
\mu &= \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d x_j, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - \mu)^2, \\
\label{eq:layer_norm_formula}
\text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}(\vx) &= \frac{\vx - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \boldsymbol{\beta},
\end{align}
where $\epsilon > 0$ is a small numerical stabilization constant, and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \R^d$ are learnable affine gain and bias parameters initialized to $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathbf{1}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}$.
\end{definition}
Because Layer Normalization operates on a per-token basis without batch-level pooling, its behavior is identical during training and evaluation, providing exceptional stability across variable-length sequences.

\paragraph{3. Position-wise Feed-Forward Networks (FFN).}
In addition to attention sublayers, each layer in the encoder and decoder contains a fully connected \textbf{Feed-Forward Network} applied to each position separately and identically:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:position_wise_ffn}
\text{\texttt{FFN}}(\vx) = \max\left(0, \vx \mW_1 + \vb_1\right) \mW_2 + \vb_2,
\end{equation}
where $\mW_1 \in \R^{\dmodel \times \dff}$, $\vb_1 \in \R^{\dff}$, $\mW_2 \in \R^{\dff \times \dmodel}$, and $\vb_2 \in \R^{\dmodel}$. Typically, the inner hidden dimension is expanded by a factor of 4 ($\dff = 4\dmodel$, e.g., $\dff = 2048$ for $\dmodel = 512$). While attention routes information across different temporal positions, the FFN provides non-linear feature transformation within each individual position.

\paragraph{1. Complete Encoder layer pipeline.}
An encoder layer $l \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ receives tensor $\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l-1)} \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}$ (with $\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(0)} = \mX_{\text{enc}}$) and applies two composite sublayers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sublayer 1 (Bidirectional Multi-Head Self-Attention)}:
    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:enc_sublayer1_attn}
    \mZ^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{MultiHead}}\left(\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l-1)}, \mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l-1)}, \mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l-1)}\right), \\
    \label{eq:enc_sublayer1_norm}
    \tilde{\mH}_{\text{enc}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}\left(\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l-1)} + \mZ^{(l)}\right).
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Sublayer 2 (Position-wise Feed-Forward Network)}:
    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:enc_sublayer2_ffn}
    \mathbf{F}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{FFN}}\left(\tilde{\mH}_{\text{enc}}^{(l)}\right), \\
    \label{eq:enc_sublayer2_norm}
    \mH_{\text{enc}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}\left(\tilde{\mH}_{\text{enc}}^{(l)} + \mathbf{F}^{(l)}\right).
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{2. Complete Decoder layer pipeline.}
A decoder layer $l \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ receives target representation $\mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l-1)} \in \R^{L_{\text{dec}} \times \dmodel}$ along with the terminal encoder output $\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(N)} \in \R^{L_{\text{enc}} \times \dmodel}$, executing three consecutive sublayers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sublayer 1 (Causal Masked Multi-Head Self-Attention)}:
    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer1_attn}
    \mZ_{\text{self}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{MaskedMultiHead}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l-1)}, \mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l-1)}, \mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l-1)}\right), \\
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer1_norm}
    \mH_{\text{dec}, 1}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l-1)} + \mZ_{\text{self}}^{(l)}\right).
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Sublayer 2 (Encoder-Decoder Multi-Head Cross-Attention)}:
    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer2_attn}
    \mZ_{\text{cross}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{MultiHead}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}, 1}^{(l)}, \mH_{\text{enc}}^{(N)}, \mH_{\text{enc}}^{(N)}\right), \\
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer2_norm}
    \mH_{\text{dec}, 2}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}, 1}^{(l)} + \mZ_{\text{cross}}^{(l)}\right).
    \end{align}
    Here, queries are projected from $\mH_{\text{dec}, 1}^{(l)}$, while keys and values are projected from $\mH_{\text{enc}}^{(N)}$.
    \item \textbf{Sublayer 3 (Position-wise Feed-Forward Network)}:
    \begin{align}
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer3_ffn}
    \mathbf{F}_{\text{dec}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{FFN}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}, 2}^{(l)}\right), \\
    \label{eq:dec_sublayer3_norm}
    \mH_{\text{dec}}^{(l)} &= \text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}\left(\mH_{\text{dec}, 2}^{(l)} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{dec}}^{(l)}\right).
    \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Post-LN vs. Pre-LN parameter stabilization.}
The formulation above represents the original \textbf{Post-LN} architecture of \cite{vaswani2017}, where normalization is placed on the residual sum: $\vy = \text{\texttt{LN}}(\vx + \mathcal{F}(\vx))$.
While mathematically elegant, deep Post-LN models require delicate learning rate warm-up schedules because gradients passing through the unnormalized residual additions can destabilize early training.
Modern implementations (such as GPT-2, LLaMA, and Mistral) predominantly adopt the \textbf{Pre-LN} formulation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pre_ln_formula}
\vy = \vx + \mathcal{F}\left(\text{\texttt{LayerNorm}}(\vx)\right),
\end{equation}
which preserves an unbroken, completely unnormalized identity shortcut throughout the entire depth of the network, enabling stable optimization without learning rate warm-up.

\section{Relative positional representations}
\label{sec:relative_positional_embeddings}

In Chapter~\ref{ch:rnn_sequence_modeling}, we introduced absolute positional encodings---both fixed sinusoidal functions \cite{vaswani2017} and learned absolute embedding vectors. Under absolute encoding, each index in the sequence ($pos = 0, 1, 2, \dots$) is assigned a dedicated vector added directly to the token embedding before entering the Transformer.

\paragraph{Motivation for relative representations.}
In natural language and software engineering source code, the precise absolute coordinate of a token is frequently less important than its \textbf{relative displacement} with respect to other tokens. For example, whether a verb appears at index 12 or index 84, its grammatical subject is typically found 2 or 3 tokens earlier.
To incorporate this inductive bias directly into the attention mechanism, Shaw et al. \cite{shaw2018} formulated \textbf{Relative Positional Representations}.

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
Instead of adding absolute vectors to the input, relative positional embeddings inject pairwise distance information directly into the scaled dot-product attention logits :
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relative_position_attention}
\text{\texttt{Attention}}(\mQ, \mK, \mV) = \softmax\left( \frac{\mQ \mK^\top + \mS_{\text{rel}}}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mV,
\end{equation}
where $\mS_{\text{rel}} \in \R^{L \times L}$ is a relative position bias matrix. For query position $i$ and key position $j$, the entry is defined by:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{rel}}[i, j] = \vq_i^\top \mathbf{a}_{\text{clip}(i - j, k)}^K,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{a}^K$ is a learned embedding representing the relative offset $i - j$, and $\text{clip}(x, k) = \max(-k, \min(k, x))$ truncates relative distances beyond a maximum clipping horizon $k$.

\paragraph{Asymmetry of relative positional embeddings: self-attention vs.~cross-attention.}
Relative positional embeddings make sense exclusively for self-attention, and cannot be naturally defined for cross-attention:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-attention}: Both queries and keys originate from the same sequence. The scalar distance $i - j$ represents a physical, well-defined offset within that sequence (e.g., token $j$ occurs 2 steps before token $i$).
    \item \textbf{Cross-attention}: Queries stem from the target sequence (e.g., generated English words in machine translation), while keys stem from the source sequence (e.g., input Italian words). Because source and target sentences possess different lengths, word orders, and grammatical alignments, there is no shared coordinate frame: asking whether an English target word is ``three positions ahead'' of an Italian source word is mathematically meaningless. Consequently, cross-attention cannot leverage relative positional biases.
\end{itemize}

\section{Parallelization advantages and computational complexity}
\label{sec:computational_complexity}

To appreciate why the Transformer superseded Recurrent Neural Networks, we analyze three foundational computational metrics: computational complexity per layer, sequential operations, and maximum signal path length \cite{vaswani2017}, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:layer_complexity_comparison}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXXX}

\textbf{Layer type} & \textbf{Complexity per layer} & \textbf{Sequential operations} & \textbf{Maximum path length} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Self-Attention} & $\mathcal{O}(L^2 \cdot d)$ & $\mathcal{O}(1)$ & $\mathcal{O}(1)$ \\

\paragraph{1. Sequential operations: shattering the recurrence barrier.}
In an RNN, computing the representation at step $L$ requires traversing an unbroken chain of $L$ sequential state updates ($\mathcal{O}(L)$ sequential operations).
In Self-Attention, computing all pairwise dot products $\mQ \mK^\top$ and value weighted sums involves dense matrix multiplications that execute in a single parallel step ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ sequential operations) across GPU tensor cores.

\paragraph{2. Maximum path length: constant-time gradient propagation.}
In recurrent networks, information and backpropagation gradients traveling between token $1$ and token $L$ must traverse $L$ intermediate state transitions. As proven by the Jacobian product $\prod_{k=t+1}^T \frac{\partial \vh_k}{\partial \vh_{k-1}}$ in Chapter~\ref{ch:rnn_sequence_modeling}, long path lengths cause exponential gradient decay.
In Self-Attention, the distance between any arbitrary pair of tokens $(i, j)$ is strictly $\mathcal{O}(1)$: the attention weight $\alpha_{i, j}$ directly links token $i$ to token $j$ in a single operation. This constant path length completely eliminates topological distance as an obstacle to learning long-range dependencies.

\paragraph{3. Asymptotic trade-off: sequence length vs. latent dimension.}
Self-attention incurs a quadratic time and memory complexity $\mathcal{O}(L^2 \cdot d)$ with respect to sequence length $L$.
When sequence length $L$ is smaller than embedding dimension $d$ (as was typical in early machine translation benchmarks where $L \approx 50$ and $d = 512$), $\mathcal{O}(L^2 \cdot d)$ is strictly faster than the recurrent cost $\mathcal{O}(L \cdot d^2)$. While long context windows ($L > 8192$) eventually encounter the $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ memory wall, the massive parallel speedup during training outweighs this cost for standard operational contexts.

\section{The full Encoder-Decoder paradigm: T5}
\label{sec:architectural_spectrum}

Following the introduction of the unified Encoder-Decoder Transformer in \cite{vaswani2017}, the machine learning community recognized that many tasks do not require both components simultaneously. Maintaining both stacks incurs unnecessary parameter bloat and memory overhead when the target problem involves either pure sequence transduction with distinct input and output vocabularies, or pure feature representation and bidirectional sequence understanding. Consequently, the architecture bifurcated into specialized paradigms: full Encoder-Decoder architectures and pure Encoder-Only architectures. (We will address pure autoregressive Decoder-Only architectures in the subsequent chapter dedicated to large-scale autoregressive pre-training and scaling laws).

\begin{itemize}

\paragraph{2. Critical distinction: task conditioning vs. instruction tuning.}
While T5's prefix-based behavior superficially resembles modern conversational assistants, \textbf{T5 is not instruction-tuned}.
T5 was trained strictly on fixed, rigid task prefixes. The model learns to recognize these specific keywords and route its internal representations accordingly. However, it exhibits no zero-shot generalization to unseen instructions or altered formulations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A slightly verbose variation, such as \texttt{"Can you translate English to German the following sentence: That is good"}, causes the model to break down and emit ungrammatical gibberish.
    \item Prompts requesting novel reasoning, such as \texttt{"Compute 2 + 2"}, fail completely because arithmetic was not included in its pre-training task templates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trade-offs of full encoder-decoder architectures}
\label{subsec:tradeoffs_enc_dec}
While the unified encoder-decoder architecture provides exceptional flexibility for arbitrary sequence transduction, maintaining both an encoder and an autoregressive decoder introduces significant computational and representational overhead when the downstream task does not involve sequence generation. In sequence classification, token tagging, or representation retrieval, running an autoregressive decoder step-by-step is completely wasteful. This motivates isolating the encoder stack into a standalone, deeply bidirectional architecture.

\section{The Encoder-Only paradigm: BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)}
\label{sec:encoder_only_bert}

When the objective is sequence understanding, document classification, or semantic code search rather than autoregressive text generation, the decoder stack is completely superfluous. Devlin et al. \cite{devlin2019} isolated the bidirectional encoder stack to create \textbf{BERT} (Figure~\ref{fig:slide86_encoder_only_bert}).

To master BERT from first principles, it is essential to systematically decompose and analyze each of its core architectural and training components:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input representation}: Why we must analyze the tripartite embedding construction (Token, Segment, Position) and special tokens (\texttt{[CLS]}, \texttt{[SEP]}), which enables a single unified Transformer stack to ingest either isolated sentences or structured sentence pairs without architectural modifications.
    \item \textbf{Masked Language Model (MLM)}: Why we must analyze bidirectional Cloze pre-training, resolving the fundamental paradox that standard autoregressive next-token prediction causes trivial label leakage in bidirectional self-attention networks.
    \item \textbf{Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)}: Why intra-sentence bidirectional token conditioning alone is insufficient for discourse-level relational reasoning, requiring explicit cross-sentence relationship pre-training.
    \item \textbf{Attention map anatomy and emergent linguistic patterns}: Why we must inspect the empirical behavior across the 144 attention heads, discovering how self-attention spontaneously organizes into syntactic dependency trackers, coreference resolvers, and delimiter registers without supervised parse trees.
    \item \textbf{Downstream fine-tuning mechanisms}: Why we must formalize the adaptation mechanics (sequence-level pooling vs. token-level classification heads), demonstrating how pre-trained bidirectional representations transfer to diverse task topologies with minimal task-specific parameters.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Input representation and special tokens}
\label{subsec:bert_input_representation}

\paragraph{Motivation for analyzing input representation.}
A central challenge in designing a universal sequence understanding backbone is handling diverse downstream input topologies: single text sequences (e.g., sentiment analysis, spam detection), paired sequences with asymmetric roles (e.g., Question Answering premise-hypothesis pairs), and span-level entities. Rather than designing disparate input pipelines for each task type, BERT unifies all inputs into a single continuous stream. Analyzing this construction reveals how lexical identity, sentence membership, and ordinal sequence position are simultaneously communicated to permutation-invariant self-attention layers.

BERT supports pairs of sentences $(A, B)$ packed into a single sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:slide88_bert_input_encoding}). Every input token representation is constructed by summing three distinct embedding vectors:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_input_embedding_sum}
\vx_i = \text{\texttt{TokenEmbedding}}(w_i) + \text{\texttt{SegmentEmbedding}}(s_i) + \text{\texttt{PositionEmbedding}}(i),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Token embeddings}: Subword tokens derived from WordPiece tokenization (with vocabulary cardinality $|\vocab| = 30{,}522$).
    \item \textbf{Segment embeddings}: Distinguishes whether the token belongs to Sentence $A$ ($E_A$) or Sentence $B$ ($E_B$). This allows self-attention queries to identify cross-sentence relationships and apply sentence-specific relational transformations.
    \item \textbf{Position embeddings}: Learned absolute positional vectors ($E_0, \dots, E_{511}$) that inject spatial sequence ordering.
\end{itemize}

BERT introduces two canonical special tokens:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{[CLS]} (\textbf{Classification}): Always prepended as the very first token ($i = 0$). Because self-attention is bidirectional, the final hidden state $\vh_{[\text{CLS}]} \in \R^{\dmodel}$ aggregates context from the entire sequence and serves as the pooled representation for sentence-level classification.
    \item \texttt{[SEP]} (\textbf{Separator}): Appended to mark the boundary between Sentence $A$ and Sentence $B$, as well as the termination of the sequence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-training task 1: Masked Language Model (MLM)}
\label{subsec:bert_masked_lm}

\paragraph{Motivation for analyzing the MLM objective.}
Analyzing the MLM objective is crucial because bidirectional representations cannot be pre-trained using standard autoregressive next-token prediction. In a bidirectional self-attention model, token $i$ can attend to all tokens $j > i$. If tasked with predicting $x_{i+1}$, the network would simply route attention to the next position and trivial identity-copy the answer, collapsing gradient learning. The MLM task formulates a non-trivial, label-leakage-free self-supervised objective inspired by the Cloze procedure \cite{devlin2019}.

In each input sequence, a subset $\mathcal{M} \subset \{1, 2, \dots, L\}$ representing approximately $15\%$ of all input tokens is uniformly selected to provide pre-training supervision. However, how should these selected positions be presented to the network?

\paragraph{The general motivation: resolving the pre-training vs. fine-tuning mismatch.}
The naive Cloze procedure would simply replace every selected token in $\mathcal{M}$ with the artificial marker \texttt{[MASK]}. While conceptually straightforward, this naive strategy introduces a fatal structural flaw known as the \textbf{pre-training vs. fine-tuning discrepancy} (or domain shift):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Absence of \texttt{[MASK]} in downstream tasks}: When BERT is subsequently fine-tuned on real-world applications (e.g., sequence classification, sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, or question answering), the input text consists exclusively of genuine natural language tokens. The artificial delimiter \texttt{[MASK]} \emph{never appears during inference}.
    \item \textbf{Pathological representation specialization}: If the encoder were exposed to \texttt{[MASK]} whenever a prediction was required, its internal self-attention heads would learn a degenerative heuristic: treat all regular words as unquestioned, static context, and only activate deep predictive feature extraction when encountering the explicit \texttt{[MASK]} token. Consequently, during fine-tuning, the network would fail to generate optimal contextual representations for normal tokens.
    \item \textbf{Neglect of the observed token}: If target positions are always overwritten with a mask symbol, the model never learns to evaluate or preserve the identity of the token physically occupying position $i$.
\end{enumerate}

To force the model to build high-quality contextual representations for \emph{all} tokens---while keeping the model uncertain about which positions are being graded---Devlin et al. \cite{devlin2019} formulated the \textbf{80/10/10 corruption policy}. Once the $15\%$ target positions $\mathcal{M}$ are chosen, they are perturbed according to three distinct operations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The 80\% replacement with \texttt{[MASK]} (Cloze semantic reconstruction)}:
    In $80\%$ of the selected positions ($12\%$ of total sequence tokens), the original token is replaced with \texttt{[MASK]}:
    $$\text{\texttt{"my dog is cute"}} \longrightarrow \text{\texttt{"my [MASK] is cute}}.$$
    \emph{Motivation}: This forms the core Cloze engine. Because the ground-truth token is physically removed from the input stream, the model cannot copy it. It is compelled to synthesize bidirectional semantic and syntactic clues from the entire left and right contexts (e.g., using \texttt{"my"} and \texttt{"is cute"} to infer a domestic pet like \texttt{"dog"}).

    \item \textbf{The 10\% replacement with a random token (Denoising and contextual validation)}:
    In $10\%$ of the selected positions ($1.5\%$ of total sequence tokens), the target token is replaced with an arbitrary token drawn uniformly from the vocabulary $w_{\text{rand}} \sim \vocab$:
    $$\text{\texttt{"my dog is cute"}} \longrightarrow \text{\texttt{"my apple is cute}}.$$
    \emph{Motivation}: If the model only ever saw \texttt{[MASK]} or correct words, it would assume that every non-masked word in the sentence is guaranteed to be 100\% truthful and contextually valid. By injecting random corrupted words, self-attention heads are forced to act as an active \textbf{anomaly detector} and \textbf{denoiser}. When the encoder encounters \texttt{"apple"} framed by \texttt{"my"} and \texttt{"is cute"}, it must detect that \texttt{"apple"} creates a semantic contradiction, discount the corrupted surface form, and reconstruct the true underlying concept (\texttt{"dog"}). This prevents the network from blindly trusting unmasked tokens.

    \item \textbf{The 10\% retention of the original token (Grounding and representation anchoring)}:
    In the remaining $10\%$ of selected positions ($1.5\%$ of total sequence tokens), the token is kept strictly unchanged:
    $$\text{\texttt{"my dog is cute"}} \longrightarrow \text{\texttt{"my dog is cute}}.$$
    \emph{Motivation}: If target positions were \emph{always} altered (either masked or replaced with random noise), the encoder would learn that the input symbol at target position $i$ is never the right answer, incentivizing it to completely discard the token's own embedding. By leaving the correct word intact 10\% of the time, the model is taught that the observed word may well be genuine. This forces the final hidden representation $\vh_i$ to remain faithfully anchored to the actual lexical token observed at that position, directly bridging the gap to downstream fine-tuning where all input tokens are real.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The global awareness effect.}
Because of this tri-fold distribution, when BERT computes self-attention across any token $x_i$, it operates under total uncertainty:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The network does not know whether token $x_i$ is one of the supervised targets being evaluated by cross-entropy loss.
    \item If $x_i$ is a target, the network does not know whether it represents an uncorrupted word, an anomalous impostor, or an explicit mask.
\end{itemize}
This deliberate ambiguity eliminates any possibility of lazy shortcut heuristics, compelling the Transformer encoder to maintain an expressive, contextually verified representation $\vh_i$ for \textbf{every single token} across the entire sequence.

For each selected position $i \in \mathcal{M}$, the topmost encoder output $\vh_i \in \R^{\dmodel}$ is projected onto the full vocabulary $\vocab$ through a linear classification layer parameterized by $\mW_{\text{MLM}} \in \R^{|\vocab| \times \dmodel}$ (whose weights are tied with the input token embedding matrix $\mE_{\text{tok}}$) and bias $\vb_{\text{MLM}} \in \R^{|\vocab|}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_mlm_prediction_prob}
P(w \mid \tilde{\mX}; \theta) = \frac{\exp(\vw_w^\top \vh_i + b_w)}{\sum_{v \in \vocab} \exp(\vw_v^\top \vh_i + b_v)}, \quad \forall w \in \vocab,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mX}$ denotes the corrupted input sequence. The MLM pre-training objective minimizes the cross-entropy loss aggregated across all masked positions:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_mlm_cross_entropy_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P(w_i^* \mid \tilde{\mX}; \theta),
\end{equation}
where $w_i^*$ is the original ground-truth token at position $i$.

\subsection{Pre-training task 2: Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)}
\label{subsec:bert_nsp}

\paragraph{Motivation for analyzing the NSP objective.}
While the Masked Language Model objective forces the encoder to capture deep intra-sentence lexical and syntactic relationships, many critical downstream tasks---such as Question Answering (QA), Natural Language Inference (NLI), and code-comment retrieval---depend fundamentally on understanding logical coherence across \emph{pairs} of sentences. Analyzing NSP explains how the network develops cross-sentence relational awareness, and motivates why subsequent architectures (e.g., RoBERTa) later audited whether NSP was truly necessary or suboptimal.

During corpus pre-training, input sequences are constructed by sampling pairs of sentences $(A, B)$ concatenated with segment markers (Figure~\ref{fig:slide90_bert_nsp}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{50\% of the time (Label $y=1$, IsNext)}: Sentence $B$ is the actual contiguous sentence immediately succeeding Sentence $A$ in the source document.
    \item \textbf{50\% of the time (Label $y=0$, NotNext)}: Sentence $B$ is a random sentence sampled independently from an entirely unrelated document in the pre-training corpus.
\end{itemize}

To classify the sentence pair without introducing an ad-hoc pooling mechanism, BERT leverages the special leading token representation $\vh_{[\text{CLS}]} \in \R^{\dmodel}$. Because self-attention is bidirectional, $\vh_{[\text{CLS}]}$ computes attention over every token in both Sentence $A$ and Sentence $B$. This pooled vector is projected through a non-linear dense transformation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_pooled_cls_representation}
\vh_{\text{pool}} = \tanh(\mW_{\text{pool}} \vh_{[\text{CLS}]} + \vb_{\text{pool}}), \quad \mW_{\text{pool}} \in \R^{\dmodel \times \dmodel}, \; \vb_{\text{pool}} \in \R^{\dmodel}.
\end{equation}
The binary prediction probability is emitted by a logistic regression head:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_nsp_probability}
\hat{y}_{\text{NSP}} = \sigma(\vw_{\text{NSP}}^\top \vh_{\text{pool}} + b_{\text{NSP}}), \quad \vw_{\text{NSP}} \in \R^{\dmodel}, \; b_{\text{NSP}} \in \R,
\end{equation}
and evaluated under the binary cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_nsp_binary_cross_entropy}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}}(\theta) = - y \log \hat{y}_{\text{NSP}} - (1 - y) \log (1 - \hat{y}_{\text{NSP}}).
\end{equation}

The unified pre-training objective jointly optimizes the composite loss across all sampled sequences:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_total_pretraining_objective}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BERT}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}}(\theta).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Attention map anatomy and emergent linguistic structures}
\label{subsec:bert_attention_map_anatomy}

\paragraph{Motivation for analyzing attention maps.}
Analyzing internal attention maps demystifies the ``black box'' of deep Transformer encoders. It allows researchers to verify whether the statistical optimizations of MLM and NSP genuinely induce human-like linguistic and syntactic competencies, or whether the model merely relies on shallow statistical surface correlations. By visualizing the attention matrices $\mA^{(l, h)}$, we observe how individual attention heads autonomously specialize into interpretable linguistic operators.

A standard BERT-Base model comprises $L = 12$ stacked encoder layers with $h = 12$ attention heads per layer, operating across a hidden dimension of $\dmodel = 768$ ($d_k = \dmodel / h = 64$). Consequently, for any input sequence, the network computes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bert_total_attention_heads}
12 \times 12 = 144 \text{ distinct attention heads (and corresponding attention maps)}.
\end{equation}
For an input sequence of length $N$, each attention head generates an $N \times N$ row-stochastic attention weight matrix $\mA^{(l, h)} \in [0, 1]^{N \times N}$ satisfying $\sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij}^{(l, h)} = 1$.

Consider the canonical linguistic probe evaluated in \cite{clark2019what}:
\begin{center}
\texttt{[CLS] The dog ate the food because it was hungry [SEP]} \quad ($N = 11 \text{ tokens}$).
\end{center}
Across the 144 attention maps, empirical probing reveals that self-attention layers autonomously partition into highly specialized linguistic registers without explicit syntactic supervision (Figure~\ref{fig:bert_attention_maps_specialization}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Coreference and anaphora resolution (intermediate layers, e.g., Layer 5, Head 4)}:
    Specific attention heads in the middle layers specialize in resolving pronominal anaphora across clausal boundaries. In the running example, when computing attention from the pronoun query token \texttt{"it"}, the head routes over $82\%$ of its total attention mass directly to the antecedent noun key \texttt{"dog"} ($\alpha = 0.82$). This allows the contextual representation of \texttt{"it"} to absorb the semantic properties of the entity it replaces.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic dependency tracking (early-to-intermediate layers)}:
    Distinct heads capture grammatical relations: verb-object heads route strong attention from transitive verbs to their direct objects (e.g., \texttt{"ate"} $\to$ \texttt{"food"} with $\alpha = 0.74$), determiner heads link articles to head nouns (\texttt{"The"} $\to$ \texttt{"dog"}, \texttt{"the"} $\to$ \texttt{"food"}), and predicate heads connect adjectives to sentence subjects (\texttt{"hungry"} $\to$ \texttt{"dog"}).
    \item \textbf{Surface sequential tracking (early layers)}:
    In early layers, several heads attend predominantly to adjacent tokens ($i \to i-1$ or $i \to i+1$), preserving localized $n$-gram ordering within the permutation-invariant self-attention matrix.
    \item \textbf{The delimiter and neutral register phenomenon (deep layers, e.g., Layer 10, Head 8)}:
    In deeper layers, Clark et al. \cite{clark2019what} and Michel et al. \cite{michel2019sixteen} observed that multiple attention heads allocate between $50\%$ and $90\%$ of their total attention weight directly to the special delimiter token \texttt{[SEP]}.
    This behavior arises from a fundamental mathematical constraint: the softmax operator strictly enforces $\sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij} = 1$. When a specialized attention head (such as a coreference head) processes a token that does not participate in that specific linguistic relation (e.g., the conjunction \texttt{"because"} or punctuation tokens), the head must still distribute a probability mass of $1.0$. Distributing this mass across arbitrary semantic words would inject irrelevant noise and distort their representations. Therefore, the network designates \texttt{[SEP]} as a \textbf{neutral default register / "no-op" target}: unassigned attention is dumped onto \texttt{[SEP]}, leaving the contextual representation of the source token virtually unaltered ($\Delta \vh \approx \mathbf{0}$).
\end{enumerate}

The emergent patterns across the layers decompose into three canonical phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Early layers (Layers 1--3: Surface and Local N-gram Geometry)}:
    As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bert_layer1_head1_attention}, attention weights are concentrated heavily along the main diagonal ($i \to i$) and adjacent off-diagonals ($i \to i-1$ and $i \to i+1$). The model first reconstructs sequential positional adjacency, functioning much like convolutional filters with small receptive fields. Furthermore, basic lexical dependencies begin forming (e.g., \texttt{"ate"} linking with \texttt{"food"}).
    \item \textbf{Middle layers (Layers 4--8: Syntactic Trees and Anaphora Resolution)}:
    In the intermediate layers, attention patterns become markedly sparse and structured (Figure~\ref{fig:bert_coreference_detail}). The heads no longer attend to adjacent words; instead, they bridge long-range syntactic gaps. Dedicated heads track verb-object dependencies, noun-modifier bindings, and pronoun coreferences across subordinate conjunctions (\texttt{"it"} $\to$ \texttt{"dog"}). This proves that the multi-head mechanism divides labor: different heads track different syntactic relations concurrently.
    \item \textbf{Deep layers (Layers 9--12: High-Level Task Abstraction and Delimiter Pooling)}:
    In the final layers, attention distributions shift again. Many heads show broad, diffuse patterns, while a significant fraction dump $80\%\text{--}90\%$ of their attention mass onto the special token \texttt{[SEP]}. Because the final layers synthesize task-level features, tokens that have already aggregated their required syntactic context utilize \texttt{[SEP]} as an inert sink. This preserves their accumulated representation vectors without absorbing unwanted semantic drift in the topmost layers before classification or MLM loss computation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Downstream fine-tuning mechanisms}
\label{subsec:bert_downstream_finetuning}

\paragraph{Motivation for analyzing fine-tuning mechanisms.}
A pre-trained foundation model is only as valuable as its adaptability to real-world tasks. Analyzing BERT's downstream adaptation demonstrates the paradigm shift from task-specific feature engineering to task-agnostic representation fine-tuning. By modifying only the topmost prediction layer while allowing gradients to backpropagate through all 110 million parameters, BERT achieves state-of-the-art performance across diverse natural language tasks with minimal training time.

Following pre-training on MLM and NSP, the generic pre-training classification layers are discarded. The pre-trained Transformer weights are retained, and minimal task-specific projection heads are appended to adapt the model to downstream NLP applications with end-to-end gradient updates:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sequence-level classification} (e.g., sentiment analysis, code defect prediction, natural language inference):
    The pooled sentence representation $\vh_{\text{pool}} \in \R^{\dmodel}$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:bert_pooled_cls_representation} is projected through a task-specific linear head:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bert_finetune_seq_classification}
    \hat{\vy} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mW_{\text{seq}} \vh_{\text{pool}} + \vb_{\text{seq}}), \quad \mW_{\text{seq}} \in \R^{C \times \dmodel}, \; \vb_{\text{seq}} \in \R^C,
    \end{equation}
    where $C$ denotes the number of target classes. All parameters (both $\mW_{\text{seq}}$ and the encoder backbone) are fine-tuned via cross-entropy loss.
    \item \textbf{Token-level sequence labeling} (e.g., Named Entity Recognition NER, Part-of-Speech tagging, code token vulnerability tagging):
    Instead of pooling, the final contextual representation $\vh_i \in \R^{\dmodel}$ of each individual token $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, L\}$ is mapped to a discrete label distribution:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bert_finetune_token_classification}
    \hat{\vy}_i = \operatorname{softmax}(\mW_{\text{tok}} \vh_i + \vb_{\text{tok}}), \quad \mW_{\text{tok}} \in \R^{K \times \dmodel}, \; \vb_{\text{tok}} \in \R^K,
    \end{equation}
    where $K$ is the number of entity or syntactic tags.
    \item \textbf{Extractive Question Answering} (e.g., SQuAD):
    Conditioned on a passage and a question concatenated as Sentence $A$ and Sentence $B$, the model identifies the continuous answer span by learning a start vector $\vw_{\text{start}} \in \R^{\dmodel}$ and an end vector $\vw_{\text{end}} \in \R^{\dmodel}$. The probability that token $i$ is the start of the answer span is:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bert_squad_start_prob}
    P_{\text{start}}(i) = \frac{\exp(\vw_{\text{start}}^\top \vh_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^L \exp(\vw_{\text{start}}^\top \vh_j)},
    \end{equation}
    with an identical formulation for $P_{\text{end}}(i)$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Architectural transition: from bidirectional representations to autoregressive generation.}
While the Encoder-Decoder paradigm addresses sequence transduction and the Encoder-Only paradigm excels at bidirectional representation learning and sequence classification, pure autoregressive generative tasks (such as open-ended text synthesis and conversational reasoning) rely on Decoder-Only architectures. We defer the comprehensive architectural analysis of large-scale Decoder-Only models to Chapter~6, where scaling laws and autoregressive pre-training are developed in full detail.

However, regardless of whether generation occurs within the decoder stack of an Encoder-Decoder model or a dedicated autoregressive language decoder, the fundamental problem of selecting discrete tokens from continuous output distributions remains mathematically invariant. In the subsequent section, we formalize the probabilistic mechanics governing autoregressive decoding.


\section{Autoregressive decoding and sampling strategies}
\label{sec:decoding_and_sampling}

At each step $t$ of autoregressive sequence generation, an autoregressive language decoder projects its topmost contextual hidden state $\vh_t \in \R^{\dmodel}$ through an unembedding linear transformation $\mW_U \in \R^{|\vocab| \times \dmodel}$ parameterized with vocabulary bias $\vb_U \in \R^{|\vocab|}$. This maps the continuous latent manifold into raw unnormalized affinities over discrete token space:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unembedding_projection_formal}
\vz_t = \mW_U \vh_t + \vb_U \in \R^{|\vocab|},
\end{equation}
where $\vz_t = [z_{t, 1}, z_{t, 2}, \dots, z_{t, |\vocab|}]^\top \in \R^{|\vocab|}$ denotes the unnormalized log-affinity (logit) vector across the vocabulary $\vocab = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_{|\vocab|}\}$. Passing $\vz_t$ through the canonical softmax operator $\operatorname{softmax}: \R^{|\vocab|} \to \Delta^{|\vocab|-1}$ maps the continuous unbounded logits onto the standard $(|\vocab|-1)$-dimensional probability simplex $\Delta^{|\vocab|-1} = \{\vp \in \R^{|\vocab|} : p_i \ge 0, \, \sum_{i=1}^{|\vocab|} p_i = 1\}$. 

This defines the discrete categorical conditional probability mass function governing the next-token random variable $X_t \sim \operatorname{Categorical}(\vp_t)$:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:unembedding_softmax_distribution}
P(X_t = v_i \mid w_{<t}) &= [\operatorname{softmax}(\vz_t)]_i \notag \\
&= \frac{\exp(z_{t, i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\vocab|} \exp(z_{t, j})} \notag \\
&= \frac{\exp\bigl(\vw_{U, i}^\top \vh_t + b_{U, i}\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\vocab|} \exp\bigl(\vw_{U, j}^\top \vh_t + b_{U, j}\bigr)}, \quad \forall v_i \in \vocab,
\end{align}
where $\vw_{U, i}^\top \in \R^{1 \times \dmodel}$ denotes the $i$-th row vector of the unembedding matrix $\mW_U$ (often tied with the input embedding matrix $\mE_{\text{tok}}^\top$ in weight-tied models), and $b_{U, i} \in \R$ represents the corresponding vocabulary bias term. By construction, the probabilities satisfy the Kolmogorov axioms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:categorical_simplex_properties}
P(X_t = v_i \mid w_{<t}) \in (0, 1) \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{|\vocab|} P(X_t = v_i \mid w_{<t}) = 1.
\end{equation}
In modern language models, the vocabulary cardinality $|\vocab|$ spans tens of thousands of subword units (for example, $|\vocab| = 30{,}522$ in BERT's WordPiece, $|\vocab| = 50{,}257$ in GPT-2's Byte-Pair Encoding). Consequently, $\vp_t$ represents a massive, highly non-uniform probability distribution over tens of thousands of discrete outcomes. 

Once this dense probability distribution $P(w_i \mid w_{<t})$ is obtained, the fundamental challenge of autoregressive inference arises: \textbf{how do we select the discrete token $\hat{w}_t$ to append to the generated sequence?} The choice of decoding policy governs the trade-off between deterministic precision and stochastic diversity, directly deciding whether the model produces factual truth, creative narrative, generic clichés, or ungrammatical gibberish.

To establish an identical, controlled empirical testbed across all decoding policies studied in this section, we condition each strategy on the opening prompt drawn from Arthur Conan Doyle's \emph{The Red-Headed League} (as examined in lecture):
\begin{quote}
\emph{``I had called upon my friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, one day in the autumn of last year and found him in deep conversation with a very stout, florid-faced, elderly gentleman with fiery red hair. With an apology for my intrusion, I was about to withdraw when Holmes pulled me abruptly into the room and closed the door behind me.''}
\end{quote}

In the following subsections, we analyze the six canonical decoding paradigms in strict linear progression: from deterministic greedy selection and beam optimization to stochastic sampling, entropy modulation, and dynamic truncation filters.

\subsection{Greedy decoding (argmax) and the degeneration trap}
\label{subsec:greedy_decoding}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
The simplest and computationally most direct decoding policy is \textbf{Greedy Search}. At each temporal step $t$, the algorithm deterministically selects the single token assigned the highest instantaneous conditional probability:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:greedy_selection}
\hat{w}_t = \operatorname{argmax}_{w \in \vocab} P(w \mid w_{<t}).
\end{equation}
Greedy decoding requires zero hyperparameters and incurs minimal computational overhead: it performs a single $\mathcal{O}(|\vocab|)$ reduction across the logit vector at each time step.

Suppose after processing the prompt, the model emits the normalized probabilities across a representative candidate subset shown in Table~\ref{tab:greedy_example}.

\paragraph{The failure mode: mathematical myopia and cyclic repetition.}
Despite its superficial appeal, greedy decoding exhibits two catastrophic failure modes in open-ended language generation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Myopia (Local Greediness $\neq$ Global Sequence Optimality)}: Sequence likelihood factorizes via the probabilistic chain rule as $P(w_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. A greedy local maximum at step $t$ frequently leads into a barren probability subspace at subsequent steps:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:greedy_myopia_divergence}
    \operatorname{argmax}_{w_1, \dots, w_T} \prod_{t=1}^T P(w_t \mid w_{<t}) \ne \prod_{t=1}^T \operatorname{argmax}_{w_t} P(w_t \mid w_{<t}).
    \end{equation}
    As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:greedy_search_failure}, committing to token $A$ because $P(A) = 0.60 > P(B) = 0.40$ traps the generator if $A$ can only be followed by low-probability tokens ($P(C \mid A) = 0.10$, yielding path likelihood $0.06$), whereas selecting the locally sub-optimal token $B$ uncovers a highly fluent sequence ($P(D \mid B) = 0.80$, yielding path likelihood $0.32$).
    \item \textbf{Neural Text Degeneration}: Holtzman et al.~\cite{holtzman2020} demonstrated that greedy decoding on neural language models triggers self-reinforcing attractor loops. Common syntactic markers (e.g., pronouns, auxiliary verbs) dominate local argmax selections. Once a repetitive n-gram is appended to the context, self-attention attending back to those identical tokens artificially elevates their conditional probabilities even higher at subsequent steps, locking the model into an inescapable loop of cyclic degeneration:
    \begin{quote}
    \texttt{"I am sorry, Mr. Holmes," I said. "I am sorry, Mr. Holmes, for having been so rude to you. I am sorry, Mr. Holmes, for having been so rude to you. I am sorry, Mr. Holmes, for having been so rude to you..."}
    \end{quote}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Causal bridge to Beam Search.}

\subsection{Beam search: exploring parallel hypotheses}
\label{subsec:beam_search}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
\textbf{Beam Search} avoids the strict myopia of greedy decoding by maintaining a fixed set of $B$ top candidate sequences (termed the \emph{beam width} $B$, or $k$ in literature) at each time step.

Let $\mathcal{Y}_t = \{\vy_t^{(1)}, \vy_t^{(2)}, \dots, \vy_t^{(B)}\}$ denote the set of active partial hypotheses of length $t$. At generation step $t+1$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Every hypothesis $\vy_t^{(b)} \in \mathcal{Y}_t$ is expanded across all $|\vocab|$ vocabulary candidates, forming $B \times |\vocab|$ child candidate sequences $\vy_{t+1} = (\vy_t^{(b)}, w)$.
    \item The cumulative score of each sequence is evaluated in logarithmic space to prevent numerical underflow:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:beam_search_log_score}
    \text{Score}(\vy_{1:t+1}) = \sum_{i=1}^{t+1} \log P(y_i \mid \vy_{<i}).
    \end{equation}
    \item The expanded set of $B \cdot |\vocab|$ candidates is sorted, and only the top-$B$ candidates with the highest cumulative log-probabilities are preserved:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:beam_search_prune}
    \mathcal{Y}_{t+1} = \operatorname{argtop-B}_{\vy \in \mathcal{Y}_t \times \vocab} \text{Score}(\vy).
    \end{equation}
    All other $B \cdot (|\vocab|-1)$ hypotheses are permanently pruned.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Length penalty normalization.}
Because $\log P(y_i \mid \vy_{<i}) \le 0$ for all valid probabilities, the objective in Equation~\eqref{eq:beam_search_log_score} is strictly non-increasing with sequence length. Consequently, unconstrained beam search inherently penalizes longer generations, favoring unnaturally short, truncated outputs. To counteract this bias, standard implementations incorporate a length normalization heuristic:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beam_length_penalty}
\text{Score}_{\text{norm}}(Y) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{|Y|} \log P(y_i \mid \vy_{<i})}{\operatorname{LP}(Y)}, \quad \text{where} \quad \operatorname{LP}(Y) = \frac{(5 + |Y|)^\alpha}{(5 + 1)^\alpha},
\end{equation}
with length penalty parameter $\alpha \in [0.6, 1.0]$. When $\alpha = 0$, no length normalization occurs; when $\alpha = 1.0$, the score is normalized by length.

\paragraph{Worked numerical example.}
Consider beam search with beam width $B=2$ expanding from the root token \texttt{"The"}, exactly replicating the lecture derivation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Step $t=1$}: From \texttt{"The"}, candidate probabilities are:
    \begin{equation}
    P(\texttt{"nice"} \mid \texttt{"The"}) = 0.50, \quad P(\texttt{"dog"} \mid \texttt{"The"}) = 0.40, \quad P(\texttt{"car"} \mid \texttt{"The"}) = 0.10.
    \end{equation}
    The top-$2$ beams retained are $\mathcal{Y}_1 = \{\texttt{("The", "nice")}, \texttt{("The", "dog")}\}$. The branch \texttt{"car"} is pruned.
    \item \textbf{Step $t=2$}: Each of the 2 active beams expands its candidates:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item From \texttt{"The dog"}: \texttt{"has"} ($P=0.90 \implies \text{joint } 0.40 \times 0.90 = 0.36$), \texttt{"and"} ($P=0.05 \implies \text{joint } 0.02$), \texttt{"run"} ($P=0.05 \implies \text{joint } 0.02$).
        \item From \texttt{"The nice"}: \texttt{"woman"} ($P=0.40 \implies \text{joint } 0.50 \times 0.40 = 0.20$), \texttt{"house"} ($P=0.30 \implies \text{joint } 0.15$), \texttt{"guy"} ($P=0.30 \implies \text{joint } 0.15$).
    \end{itemize}
    Across all 6 expanded paths, the top-$2$ global sequences are \texttt{"The dog has"} ($P=0.36$) and \texttt{"The nice woman"} ($P=0.20$). All other 4 paths are pruned.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The failure mode of beam search in open-ended generation.}
While beam search is the gold standard for closed, goal-oriented tasks with narrow target spaces (such as neural machine translation and formal code generation), it fails strikingly in open-ended creative text generation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Blandness and Clichés}: Human communication does not optimize for maximum likelihood at every step. Natural human language contains an alternating rhythm of predictable syntactic connectors (low information entropy) and surprising, domain-specific words (high information entropy). Maximizing cumulative likelihood forces the model into high-probability clichés and generic sentences.
    \item \textbf{Repetitive Stagnation}: Holtzman et al.~\cite{holtzman2020} demonstrated that increasing the beam width $B$ in open-ended language generation actually \emph{degrades} perceived quality: as $B \to \infty$, the search uncovers pathological repeating loops that accumulate high probability mass over extended horizons.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Causal bridge to Random Sampling.}
To escape deterministic clichés and inject human-like entropy, we must transition from deterministic maximization to \textbf{stochastic generation}. This leads directly to \textbf{Pure Random Sampling}.

% 5.11.3 RANDOM SAMPLING
% ==============================================================================
\subsection{Pure random sampling and the catastrophic tail risk}
\label{subsec:random_sampling}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
Instead of deterministically tracking top-ranking candidates, \textbf{Pure Random Sampling} (Multinomial Sampling) draws the next token directly from the categorical probability distribution generated by the softmax unembedding:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:random_multinomial_sampling}
w_t \sim \operatorname{Categorical}\left(P(w_1 \mid w_{<t}), P(w_2 \mid w_{<t}), \dots, P(w_{|\vocab|} \mid w_{<t})\right).
\end{equation}
Sampling is implemented computationally via Inverse Transform Sampling: given a uniform pseudo-random draw $u \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1)$, select index $\hat{i}$ satisfying:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inverse_transform_sampling}
\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{i}-1} P(w_j \mid w_{<t}) \le u < \sum_{j=1}^{\hat{i}} P(w_j \mid w_{<t}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Worked numerical example.}
Returning to the distribution from Table~\ref{tab:greedy_example}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under greedy search, \texttt{"I} is selected with $100\%$ probability.
    \item Under random sampling:
    \begin{equation}
    P(w_t = \texttt{"I"}) = 52.1\%, \quad P(w_t = \texttt{"He"}) = 26.0\%, \quad P(w_t = \texttt{"The"}) = 13.0\%, \quad \dots
    \end{equation}
    If the pseudo-random variable evaluates to $u = 0.650$, since $0.521 \le 0.650 < (0.521 + 0.260 = 0.781)$, the generator selects token $\hat{w}_t = \texttt{"He"}$. The generated continuation gains immediate narrative variety.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The failure mode: the unreliable long tail and hallucination cascades.}
While random sampling completely eliminates repetitive loops, it introduces a severe vulnerability: \textbf{the catastrophic tail hazard}.
In language models with $|\vocab| \approx 50{,}000$, although individual tokens in the tail have infinitesimal probabilities (e.g., $P(w) < 10^{-4}$), the \emph{cumulative aggregate mass} of the thousands of tail tokens commonly sums to $15\%$ to $30\%$ of the entire probability simplex (Figure~\ref{fig:random_sampling_tail_risk}).

\paragraph{Causal bridge to Temperature Scaling.}
Before resorting to hard truncation of the vocabulary, can we smoothly reshape the probability landscape itself, suppressing the tail while accentuating dominant modes? This leads to \textbf{Temperature Scaling}.

% ==============================================================================
\subsection{Temperature scaling: thermodynamic entropy modulation}
\label{subsec:temperature_scaling}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
\textbf{Temperature Scaling} modifies the sharpness of the categorical probability distribution by introducing a continuous thermodynamic parameter $T > 0$ that scales raw logits prior to exponentiation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temperature_softmax}
P(w_i; T) = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\vocab|} \exp(z_j / T)}, \quad \forall w_i \in \vocab.
\end{equation}
The parameter $T$ acts as a continuous entropy dial:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low Temperature ($T < 1.0$)}: Amplifies relative logit differences $z_i - z_j$, concentrating probability mass on the highest-scoring tokens (reducing Shannon entropy).
    \item \textbf{Standard Temperature ($T = 1.0$)}: Preserves the original, calibrated categorical distribution learned during maximum-likelihood pre-training.
    \item \textbf{High Temperature ($T > 1.0$)}: Dampens logit disparities, flattening the distribution toward maximum-entropy uniformity.
\end{itemize}

Consider a model evaluating three candidate tokens with raw logits $\vz = [10.0, 8.0, 5.0]^\top$. Table~\ref{tab:temperature_scaling_worked} computes the resulting probabilities across four temperature regimes.

\paragraph{Analytical limits and mathematical proofs.}
The behavior of temperature scaling is bounded by two rigorous analytical limits:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low-Temperature Limit ($T \to 0^+$, Greedy Convergence)}:
    Let $z^* = \max_j z_j$ denote the unique maximal logit. Factoring out $\exp(z^* / T)$ from the numerator and denominator of Equation~\eqref{eq:temperature_softmax}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:temperature_zero_limit}
    \lim_{T \to 0^+} P(w_i; T) = \lim_{T \to 0^+} \frac{\exp((z_i - z^*) / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\vocab|} \exp((z_j - z^*) / T)}.
    \end{equation}
    For the maximal token where $z_i = z^*$, $(z_i - z^*) / T = 0$, so $\exp(0) = 1$. For any sub-optimal token $k$ where $z_k < z^*$, the numerator satisfies $z_k - z^* = -\delta < 0$ ($\delta > 0$). As $T \to 0^+$, $-\delta / T \to -\infty$, yielding $\exp(-\delta / T) \to 0$. Therefore:
    \begin{equation}
    \lim_{T \to 0^+} P(w_i; T) = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } z_i = \max_j z_j, \\
    0 & \text{if } z_i < \max_j z_j.
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    Temperature sampling converges continuously to deterministic \textbf{Greedy decoding}.
    \item \textbf{High-Temperature Limit ($T \to \infty$, Uniform Collapse)}:
    As $T \to \infty$, for every finite logit $z_i$, the argument $z_i / T \to 0$, implying $\exp(z_i / T) \to 1$. Consequently:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:temperature_inf_limit}
    \lim_{T \to \infty} P(w_i; T) = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\vocab|} 1} = \frac{1}{|\vocab|}.
    \end{equation}
    The distribution collapses into a maximal-entropy uniform distribution across all $|\vocab|$ vocabulary tokens.
    \item \textbf{Monotonicity of Shannon Entropy}:
    The Shannon entropy of the distribution is defined as $H(P; T) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|\vocab|} P_i(T) \log P_i(T)$. Differentiating with respect to $T$ yields:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:entropy_temperature_monotonicity}
    \frac{\partial H(P; T)}{\partial T} = \frac{1}{T^3} \operatorname{Var}_{P}(z) = \frac{1}{T^3} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{|\vocab|} P_i(T) z_i^2 - \left(\sum_{i=1}^{|\vocab|} P_i(T) z_i\right)^2 \right) \ge 0.
    \end{equation}
    Because variance is strictly non-negative ($\operatorname{Var}_P(z) \ge 0$), entropy increases strictly monotonically with $T$ whenever logits are non-identical.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitation of temperature scaling.}
Although temperature scaling compresses or flattens the distribution, \textbf{it never zeros out the probability of any token}: every token in the vocabulary retains $P(w_i; T) > 0$. In low-entropy contexts where only a single token is correct, even a lowered temperature leaves non-zero probability mass in the tail, still leaving room for improbable hallucinations. Conversely, lowering temperature globally to combat tail noise also suppresses legitimate synonyms in creative passages.

\paragraph{Causal bridge to Top-$k$ Sampling.}
To guarantee that low-probability tokens cannot be sampled under any circumstances, we must apply explicit \textbf{truncation} to the support of the distribution. This motivates \textbf{Top-$k$ Sampling}.

% ==============================================================================
\subsection{Top-$k$ sampling: hard cardinality truncation}
\label{subsec:top_k_sampling}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
Introduced by Fan et al.~\cite{fan2018}, \textbf{Top-$k$ Sampling} bounds tail risk by strictly restricting the sampling candidate set to the $k$ most probable tokens at each generation step.

\begin{definition}{Top-$k$ sampling}{top_k_defn}
Let $\vocab^{(k)} \subset \vocab$ denote the subset of size $|\vocab^{(k)}| = k$ containing the tokens with the highest probabilities under $P(w \mid w_{<t})$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:top_k_subset}
\sum_{w \in \vocab^{(k)}} P(w \mid w_{<t}) \quad \text{is maximized subject to } |\vocab^{(k)}| = k.
\end{equation}
All tokens outside $\vocab^{(k)}$ have their probabilities truncated to zero. The remaining $k$ probabilities are renormalized by dividing by their cumulative mass $S_k = \sum_{w_j \in \vocab^{(k)}} P(w_j)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:top_k_renormalization}
P'(w_i) = \begin{cases}
\frac{P(w_i)}{\sum_{w_j \in \vocab^{(k)}} P(w_j)} & \text{if } w_i \in \vocab^{(k)}, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The next token is sampled from this truncated categorical distribution: $w_t \sim \operatorname{Categorical}(P')$.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{The failure mode: the structural rigidity of a static $k$.}
Despite its widespread adoption, top-$k$ sampling suffers from an inherent architectural rigidity: \textbf{a static threshold $k$ cannot adapt to varying distribution entropy across different contexts}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low-Entropy (Peaked / Factual Context)}: Suppose the prompt is \texttt{"The capital of France is"}. The model assigns $P(\texttt{"Paris"}) = 0.98$, while all other tokens are factual errors. If $k=50$, top-$k$ sampling is forced to retain $49$ improbable, factually erroneous tokens. Because their collective mass is renormalized, the chance of sampling an error is artificially elevated.
    \item \textbf{High-Entropy (Flat / Creative Context)}: Suppose the prompt is \texttt{"She looked out into the vast horizon and saw"}. Hundreds of valid English nouns and verbs (e.g., \texttt{"mountains"}, \texttt{"ships"}, \texttt{"stars"}, \texttt{"glaciers"}) share roughly equal probabilities ($\sim 0.005$). If $k=10$ or $k=20$, top-$k$ prematurely truncates dozens of perfectly fluent, highly creative candidate tokens.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Causal bridge to Top-$p$ (Nucleus) Sampling.}
To overcome the inflexibility of a fixed cardinality $k$, the candidate pool size must become \textbf{dynamic}, expanding in high-entropy situations and contracting in low-entropy situations. This motivates \textbf{Top-$p$ (Nucleus) Sampling}.

% ==============================================================================
\subsection{Top-$p$ (nucleus) sampling: dynamic cumulative mass truncation}
\label{subsec:top_p_sampling}

\paragraph{Mathematical formulation.}
To eliminate the rigid cardinality constraint of top-$k$, Holtzman et al.~\cite{holtzman2020} proposed \textbf{Top-$p$ (Nucleus) Sampling}. Instead of fixing the number of tokens $k$, nucleus sampling fixes the \textbf{cumulative probability mass threshold} $p \in (0, 1]$.

\begin{definition}{Top-$p$ (Nucleus) sampling}{top_p_defn}
Let $p \in (0, 1]$ be a cumulative probability threshold. Let $\vocab^{(p)} \subset \vocab$ denote the smallest subset of vocabulary tokens whose cumulative probability mass exceeds threshold $p$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nucleus_set_definition}
\vocab^{(p)} = \operatorname{argmin}_{V' \subseteq \vocab} |V'| \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{w \in V'} P(w \mid w_{<t}) \ge p.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, sorting tokens in descending order of probability $P(w_{(1)}) \ge P(w_{(2)}) \ge \dots \ge P(w_{(|\vocab|)})$, the nucleus cutoff index $k^*(p)$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nucleus_cutoff_index}
k^*(p) = \min \left\{ k \in \{1, \dots, |\vocab|\} \;\middle|\; \sum_{i=1}^k P(w_{(i)} \mid w_{<t}) \ge p \right\}.
\end{equation}
All tokens with rank $i > k^*(p)$ are zeroed out, and the probabilities within $\vocab^{(p)}$ are renormalized:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nucleus_renormalization}
P'(w_i) = \begin{cases}
\frac{P(w_i)}{\sum_{w_j \in \vocab^{(p)}} P(w_j)} & \text{if } w_i \in \vocab^{(p)}, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The next token is sampled from $P'$.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Worked numerical example: adaptive candidate cardinality.}
To demonstrate the dynamic adaptivity of nucleus sampling, consider setting threshold $p = 0.90$ across two different contexts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Context A (Peaked / Low Entropy)}: Prompt: \texttt{"The capital of France is"}.
    \begin{align*}
    P(\texttt{"Paris"}) &= 0.92, \quad P(\texttt{"the"}) = 0.03, \quad P(\texttt{"a"}) = 0.01, \quad \dots \\
    \text{Cumulative sum}: \quad & P(\texttt{"Paris"}) = 0.92 \ge 0.90.
    \end{align*}
    The nucleus contains \textbf{only 1 token}: $\vocab^{(p)} = \{\texttt{"Paris"}\}$. The candidate pool automatically collapses to greedy behavior, preventing hallucinations.
    \item \textbf{Context B (Flat / High Entropy)}: Prompt: \texttt{"She looked through the telescope and saw"}.
    Suppose tokens $w_{(1)}$ through $w_{(15)}$ each carry $P \approx 0.06$. To accumulate $\sum P \ge 0.90$, the nucleus retains $k^*(0.90) = 15$ tokens. The candidate pool dynamically expands, allowing rich lexical variation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Algorithmic selection guide for software engineering and NLP tasks.}
In practical production deployments, no single decoding policy dominates all use cases. The optimal strategy depends strictly on whether the downstream task prioritizes \textbf{invariance to randomness} (factual precision, exact syntax) or \textbf{stochastic diversity} (creative exploration, synthetic data generation):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Tasks (Code Generation, Unit Test Synthesis, Mathematical Reasoning, Machine Translation)}:
    Require strict reproducibility and syntactic validity. Standard practice employs \textbf{Greedy Decoding} ($T=0$) or \textbf{Beam Search} with small beam width ($B \in [2, 5]$) and length penalty $\alpha \in [0.6, 0.8]$.
    \item \textbf{Open-Ended Creative Tasks (Conversational Agents, Story Generation, Synthetic Documentation)}:
    Require human-like cadence and vocabulary richness. Standard practice employs \textbf{Top-$p$ (Nucleus) Sampling} ($p \in [0.90, 0.95]$) coupled with moderate temperature ($T \in [0.6, 0.8]$).
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Pipeline: Combining Temperature, Top-$k$, and Top-$p$}:
    Modern inference engines (e.g., vLLM, Hugging Face `transformers`) execute decoding as a multi-stage sequential filter pipeline:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:decoding_pipeline_stages}
    \vz \xrightarrow{\text{Temperature } T} \frac{\vz}{T} \xrightarrow{\text{Top-}k \text{ Truncation}} \vocab^{(k)} \xrightarrow{\text{Top-}p \text{ Nucleus}} \vocab^{(p)} \xrightarrow{\text{Softmax \& Categorical Draw}} \hat{w}_t.
    \end{equation}
    Scaling logits by $T$ first modulates relative entropy; filtering by Top-$k$ removes extreme vocabulary outliers (e.g., $k=50$); filtering by Top-$p$ dynamically adapts candidate size for the remaining distribution; and sampling from the renormalized subset provides controlled, diverse outputs.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The causal bridge to Scaling Laws.}
While encoder-only models dominated semantic representation, syntax parsing, and discriminative classification, decoder-only models introduced a profound architectural unification: their pre-training objective (autoregressive next-token prediction) is identical to their inference task.
By pairing decoder-only transformers with stochastic sampling strategies---such as nucleus sampling ($p \in [0.9, 0.95]$) and temperature scaling ($T \in [0.6, 0.8]$)---researchers uncovered an extraordinary emergent capability: as model parameter count, training token volume, and computational budget scale upward, language models exhibit few-shot and zero-shot reasoning without task-specific parameter fine-tuning.
